What Is a Burdened Labor Rate for Contractors?

September 3, 2026 · 6 min read · JobProfitCalc.com

The Maple Street office was supposed to be the clean one. Two days, one guy on the tools, a check that cleared before the dumpster left the lot. Marco ran it at $28 an hour (an example wage, not a claim about pay in your market), the owner priced the labor line at that same $28, and the job felt fine the Friday they walked out. Three weeks later the quarterly payroll tax deposit came due, and the workers' comp carrier sent a mid-year premium adjustment after the payroll report. The owner sat down with Marco's timesheets and the job folder and watched a win turn into a short ticket.

Most owners do not build a loaded rate until a week like that one. Quoting off the wage is the expensive habit, and it survives because the bill arrives weeks after the job felt fine.

The Burdened Labor Rate Is a Cost, Not a Price

What he typed was a wage. What that hour actually cost him was the wage plus everything the quarterly deposit and the comp notice turned out to contain, spread back across the hours Marco worked. That loaded figure is the burdened labor rate, and the thing to notice about it is that it is a cost, not a price.

The price is its own calculation. A charge-out hourly rate gets built from what the business needs to earn, not from what Marco's stub says, so a shop that quotes its own cost has given away the margin before the truck pulls up.

Overhead is the piece people fold in by accident. The truck, the shop, general liability, the software: those bills arrive whether Maple Street happens or not, which is why they belong in their own percentage of direct costs, walked from your own books the way what overhead percentage a contractor should use lays out. Push the pickup into Marco's hour and you inflate the labor line on every quote you write. Bury the employer payroll tax in overhead and it quietly leaves both lines.

Here is the paid-hour formula:

Burdened labor rate = (Wage + Employer payroll taxes + Workers' comp + Benefits + Other payroll burden) ÷ Paid hours

Divide those same dollars by the hours that actually hit a ticket and the cost on the tools goes up, because some of what you paid Marco never landed on that job.

What Goes Into a Fully Loaded Hour

Pull the pieces from your own payroll. A forum's "typical burden percent" is someone else's class code and someone else's benefits, which is how a precise-looking quote ends up wrong.

The wage is the only line most owners type. $28 an hour (example) is what Marco sees on the stub. This ticket stayed straight time, so the owner used $28 and stopped. If a job runs overtime, load that higher wage. The burden rides on the dollars you actually pay.

He stopped because he used to be the guy on the tools, and the only taxes he ever watched were the ones withheld from his own check. Those are Marco's share. The employer share of Social Security and Medicare, plus federal and state unemployment, never prints on that stub. A person who has only ever been an employee can go years thinking taxes are already handled, which is why the quarterly deposit felt like it came out of nowhere.

Workers' compensation landed the same week. The carrier is not insuring the truck. It is pricing the chance Marco gets hurt doing the work you sent him to do, and that price scales with payroll in his class code. Put the premium on the labor hour or it vanishes from the job that created the exposure.

Benefits are the quiet monthly ones. Health insurance the shop writes a check for, a retirement match if you run one. Convert the month into an amount per paid hour. You still write that check in a week with no tickets, so the hours that get billed have to carry it.

Paid time off works the same way, and so does the Friday Marco spent in the shop sorting material so Monday on Maple Street would start clean. You pay those hours. They do not appear on a customer's invoice. Skip them on the burden and skip them in the denominator, and you have two ways to pretend the hour is cheaper than it is.

Calculating Marco's Burdened Labor Rate on Maple Street

The figures below are an example only, invented to show the math, not a claim about pay, workers' comp in your state, or typical benefits. The owner rebuilt one paid hour of Marco the way the bills actually arrived.

Example hour (made-up numbers, not a typical rate)
Hourly wage$28.00
Employer payroll taxes (example)$2.50
Workers' compensation (example)$1.80
Health insurance and other benefits, per hour (example)$4.70
Fully loaded cost per paid hour$37.00
$28.00 + $2.50 + $1.80 + $4.70 = $37.00

That is nine dollars an hour above the wage he typed into the quote. Sixteen hours at $28 had booked $448 of labor; at $37 the labor line is $592. The $144 difference is the quarterly deposit, the benefits, and the comp premium sitting in a job price that never saw them. That $37 is still a cost, and it goes on the job as the labor line, next to materials and a separate overhead percentage.

Cost per Productive Hour: The Hours That Never Hit the Ticket

Even that $37 assumes every paid hour is a billed hour. Marco's week was not built that way. Shop time, waiting on a delivery, unbilled cleanup, the holiday Monday after this job closed: you pay them, and if you recover cost only on hours that hit a ticket, those idle hours have nowhere to go except onto the hours that did.

Take one of the two days on Maple Street. The owner paid 8 hours at the $37 loaded rate, $296. Marco was on the tools for 6.5 of them, because the morning was load-out and the last stretch was a return trip the customer was never going to see on the invoice.

Cost per productive hour = (Loaded cost per paid hour × Paid hours) ÷ Productive hours
$37.00 × 8 ÷ 6.5 ≈ $45.54

The job should have seen about $46 per hour on site, not $28, and not even the $37 paid-hour rate. If almost every paid hour is on a job, the paid-hour rate is close enough. If a lot of paid time is shop, wait, and PTO, divide the year's fully loaded labor cost by productive hours instead.

Put the Loaded Rate on the Ticket

On the next estimate, put hours × crew × the burdened rate on the labor line. For this example job that is 16 × $37 if every hour hits the ticket, or closer to the productive-hour figure if your timesheets look like Marco's.

Keep overhead as its own percentage of direct costs. Walk that rate from your overhead percentage, not from this labor stack. Folding the truck into Marco's hour is how the wage looks covered and the pickup still eats the month.

The expensive habit: typing $28 into a labor field because that is the number you know by heart. Maple Street got approved that way. The deposit did not care what the quote felt like.

Frequently Asked Questions

What if I have no employees?

If you have no W-2 payroll, you may not need this stack. You still cannot treat your own time as $0. Price your hour from the charge-out hourly rate method. If you run payroll for yourself, burden those hours the same way you would a crew member.

The workers' comp audit changed my premium after the job closed. Do I go back and reprice old work?

You cannot reprice a closed ticket. You can stop using last year's premium on next month's quotes. The audit is a trailing adjustment on payroll you already paid. The forward-looking rate is the one that belongs in the next labor line.

The Bottom Line

Maple Street felt profitable because the labor line was a wage. Load payroll taxes, workers' comp, and benefits onto that example hour and it costs $37; once unbilled paid time is in the denominator, it shakes out near $46 on the tools. Use that loaded cost on the labor line, keep overhead separate, and run the next job in the job profit calculator before the quarter closes on you.
