<section>
  <p>To price snow plowing per push, add up what your truck and blade setup cost, divide that by the pushes you expect over the seasons you plan to recover it, and charge that equipment share as a direct cost on every push. Then add the hours, drive time, fuel, overhead, and margin for the push itself. Snow plowing prices that skip the equipment share only cover the operator and the fuel. For a sense of scale, Hiniker, a plow manufacturer, lists full U.S. setups from $20,000 to $35,000 at the budget end up to $55,000 to $80,000 or more at the high end in its 2026 cost guide published June 28, 2026.</p>
  <p>The usual way a per-push price falls short is slow: the snow plow price per push covers the operator and the fuel but never carries the truck and blade, so when the blade wears out or the truck needs replacing, there is no money set aside for it. This guide walks through how to price snow plowing so the equipment pays for itself, how to check one push price against your own costs, and how to set a floor so tiny lots stop costing you money.</p>
  <p>If you are still deciding whether to sell seasonal contracts or bill each visit, that choice is covered in <a href="/blog/snow-removal-contract-vs-per-push.html">snow removal contract vs per push</a>. Everything below assumes you have chosen per-push billing for at least some of your lots.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>How many pushes will you actually get</h2>
  <p>A push here means one visit to plow one lot. If the shop plows 10 lots after a storm, that storm produced 10 pushes. Your season count is the number of storms worth plowing times the lots on your route, plus any extra visits during long storms.</p>
  <p>Pushes per season is your number, not a market number. It comes from your own records of past winters and the snowfall where you work. Nobody can hand you a correct count for your town, and this guide does not try. Every count below is an assumed example you replace with yours.</p>
  <p>The count matters more than most people expect. A warm winter means fewer pushes, so each push has to carry a bigger share of the setup cost. The truck payment does not shrink because it did not snow. If you priced the season on a big count and the snow never came, you recover only part of the equipment that year.</p>
  <p>Some practical ways to land on a count:</p>
  <ul>
    <li>Pull invoices or route logs from the last three to five winters and count actual visits per lot.</li>
    <li>Use the lower years, not the average, if you want a cushion against a thin winter.</li>
    <li>New shops without records can start from local snowfall history and a conservative guess, then correct it after the first season.</li>
    <li>Count lots you have signed, not lots you hope to sign.</li>
  </ul>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Spreading the setup across the season</h2>
  <p>The basic arithmetic is short. Divide the setup cost by the number of seasons you plan to recover it over, then divide that by the pushes you expect each season. The result is your equipment share, the cost per push of owning the truck and blade.</p>
  <p>Equipment life and recovery period have no published standard here. Pick a number of seasons you are comfortable with, based on how long your past equipment lasted and how hard you run it. Shorter recovery periods put more cost on each push and get you back to even sooner.</p>
  <p>Once you have the share, treat it the way standard job costing treats any machine that does the work: as a direct equipment cost of each push, the same as you would charge a machine rate per hour on any other job. The truck and blade are not running the office; they are on the lot doing the push, so the cost belongs on the push.</p>
  <h3>The main method: a per-push equipment line</h3>
  <p>Keep your normal Overhead % for true shop overhead (insurance, office, phone, yard, and the like), and add the equipment share as a fixed dollar amount on each push. The JobProfitCalc calculator has no separate equipment field, so enter the equipment share in the Fuel / Travel Cost field together with your fuel. The label does not match, and you should know why it still works: the calculator adds Fuel / Travel Cost to total cost at face value, after overhead and before margin. That is exactly how a direct equipment charge should behave. It is not inflated by your overhead percentage, and your margin still applies to it. Write "fuel plus equipment share" in your notes so nobody reads that number as fuel alone. The <a href="/blog/contractor-overhead-percentage.html">contractor overhead percentage</a> guide covers how to build the shop overhead number that stays in Overhead %.</p>
  <h3>The alternative: folding equipment into Overhead %</h3>
  <p>Some shops prefer not to track a separate line and instead convert the season's equipment cost into extra overhead percentage. Divide the season's equipment cost by the season's overhead base (labor, drive labor, and materials at cost across all your pushes) and add that to your normal overhead. It gives the same price on a typical push, but the overhead figure it produces looks nothing like a normal shop overhead, because it has the truck and blade loaded into it. In the examples below that is how 15% shop overhead turns into 65%, and in a thin winter into a figure above 100%, which the calculator's Overhead % field does not accept (it tops out at 100%). It also under-charges small lots, because a percentage shrinks with the job while the truck's cost per visit does not.</p>
  <h3>Pick one and stick with it</h3>
  <p>Either method works. Using both at once is the common mistake: the equipment gets counted in Overhead % and again as a per-push line, and the price climbs for no reason. Lots go to competitors, and you assume the market is cheap when the real problem is double counting. Choose one method for snow, write it on your pricing sheet, and use it every time.</p>
  <p>For most shops the per-push equipment line is the better default. It matches how equipment is normally costed, it charges the same truck cost to a small lot as to a big one, and it keeps your Overhead % a number you can compare with past years. If you do fold equipment into Overhead %, label that figure "overhead including equipment" so a 65% figure is never mistaken for ordinary shop overhead.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Pricing one push, step by step</h2>
  <ol>
    <li>Add up the setup cost from your own receipts or dealer quotes, including install.</li>
    <li>Estimate pushes per season from your records, leaning toward the low side.</li>
    <li>Work out the equipment share per push and enter it as a per-push line (or, if you use the alternative, as extra Overhead %), and write the choice down.</li>
    <li>Time one push on the lot you are pricing, from blade down to blade up, including any shovel or salt work.</li>
    <li>Run the numbers in the <a href="/calculator">calculator</a> with your loaded labor rate, drive time, fuel, materials, overhead, and margin.</li>
    <li>Set a minimum charge per push so the smallest lots still cover drive time, equipment share, and margin.</li>
    <li>Recheck at mid-season by counting the pushes you have actually made against the count you priced on.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>On step four, time it on a real visit if you can. Plow speed and lot clearing time have no reliable published figure, and lots vary too much for a rule of thumb. A lot with tight islands, cart corrals, or curbs you have to feather around can take far longer than its square footage suggests.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Worked example 1: one push on a small commercial lot</h2>
  <p>This is an example. Every input below is assumed for illustration; replace each one with your own numbers.</p>
  <h3>The equipment share</h3>
  <p>Say the shop owns a truck and straight blade with a total setup cost of $30,000, an example picked from inside Hiniker's budget setup range of $20,000 to $35,000 (2026 guide, U.S., June 28, 2026). Assume the shop plans to recover it over 5 plowing seasons. Assume 20 storms worth plowing and 10 lots on the route, for 200 pushes per season.</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th>Line</th><th>Arithmetic and result</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Equipment cost per season</td><td>$30,000.00 / 5 seasons = $6,000.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Equipment share per push</td><td>$6,000.00 / 200 pushes = $30.00</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <h3>The push itself</h3>
  <p>Assume the lot takes 0.75 hours on site with 1 worker at a loaded cost of $40.00 per hour. Assume 0.25 hours of drive time from the previous stop, $8.00 of fuel for the push and drive, and bundled salt: $20.00 of bagged salt at cost in Material Cost, since this example's push price includes salt (see <a href="/blog/how-to-price-salt-per-application.html">how to price salt per application</a> for building that figure). Assume the shop's normal overhead, without equipment, is 15%, and a 25% Desired Profit Margin. The $30.00 equipment share goes into Fuel / Travel Cost next to the $8.00 of fuel.</p>
  <p>Enter materials at your cost. Your margin already earns profit on materials. If you also want a materials markup, use the optional Material Markup field: it adds profit on top of your margin, and the calculator shows your effective margin. This example leaves Material Markup at 0.</p>
  <p>The reasoning behind markup as a separate choice is in <a href="/blog/how-to-mark-up-materials-as-a-contractor.html">how to mark up materials as a contractor</a>.</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th>Line</th><th>Arithmetic and result</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Labor</td><td>0.75 hours x 1 worker x $40.00 = $30.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Drive labor</td><td>0.25 hours x 1 worker x $40.00 = $10.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Materials at cost (bundled salt)</td><td>$20.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Overhead base</td><td>$30.00 + $10.00 + $20.00 = $60.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Overhead (shop overhead only)</td><td>15% x $60.00 = $9.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Fuel / Travel Cost (fuel plus equipment share)</td><td>$8.00 fuel + $30.00 equipment share = $38.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Total cost</td><td>$30.00 + $10.00 + $20.00 + $9.00 + $38.00 = $107.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Price</td><td>$107.00 / (1 minus 0.25) = $107.00 / 0.75 = $142.67, plus $20.00 x 0% markup = $0.00, so $142.67</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Profit</td><td>$142.67 minus $107.00 = $35.67</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Margin earned</td><td>$35.67 / $142.67 = 25.0%</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <p>The $30.00 equipment share sits in total cost at face value, and the 25% margin is earned on it like every other cost. That is the result you want from a direct equipment charge.</p>
  <h3>The same push with equipment folded into Overhead %</h3>
  <p>For the alternative method, the shop turns the season's equipment cost into extra overhead. Assume a typical push on this route has the same $60.00 overhead base. Across the season that base is 200 x $60.00 = $12,000.00. The equipment percentage is $6,000.00 / $12,000.00 = 50%, so Overhead % for snow becomes 15% + 50% = 65%, and Fuel / Travel Cost goes back to the $8.00 of fuel alone. That 65% is high only because the truck and blade are loaded into it; the shop's ordinary overhead is still 15%.</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th>Line</th><th>Arithmetic and result</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Overhead (including equipment)</td><td>65% x $60.00 = $39.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Fuel / Travel Cost</td><td>$8.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Total cost</td><td>$30.00 + $10.00 + $20.00 + $39.00 + $8.00 = $107.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Price</td><td>$107.00 / 0.75 = $142.67</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <p>Of the $39.00, $9.00 is the normal 15% and $30.00 is the 50% equipment portion, so on a push that matches the season's typical base, the two methods agree. They drift apart on lots that are much bigger or smaller than typical, which is why the per-push line is the main method here.</p>
  <p>If the shop made the double counting mistake and used the 65% overhead and also added the $30.00 line, total cost would be $137.00 and the price $182.67, which is $40.00 more for the same push with no extra cost behind it.</p>
</section>

  <h2>Worked example 2: the same lot in a warm winter</h2>
  <p>Again, every input is an assumed example. Keep everything from example 1 except the push count. Say the winter is warm and the shop expects only half the pushes: 100 instead of 200.</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th>Line</th><th>Arithmetic and result</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Equipment cost per season</td><td>$30,000.00 / 5 seasons = $6,000.00 (unchanged)</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Equipment share per push</td><td>$6,000.00 / 100 pushes = $60.00</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <p>The equipment share per push doubled from $30.00 to $60.00. Now the push, with the share in Fuel / Travel Cost and shop overhead still at 15%:</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th>Line</th><th>Arithmetic and result</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Labor</td><td>0.75 x 1 x $40.00 = $30.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Drive labor</td><td>0.25 x 1 x $40.00 = $10.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Materials at cost (bundled salt)</td><td>$20.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Overhead (shop overhead only)</td><td>15% x $60.00 = $9.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Fuel / Travel Cost (fuel plus equipment share)</td><td>$8.00 fuel + $60.00 equipment share = $68.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Total cost</td><td>$30.00 + $10.00 + $20.00 + $9.00 + $68.00 = $137.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Price</td><td>$137.00 / 0.75 = $182.67, markup $0.00, so $182.67</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Profit</td><td>$182.67 minus $137.00 = $45.67</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Margin earned</td><td>$45.67 / $182.67 = 25.0%</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <p>If you use the Overhead % alternative instead, the same thin winter shows why it gets awkward. The season's overhead base falls to 100 pushes x $60.00 = $6,000.00, so the equipment percentage becomes $6,000.00 / $6,000.00 = 100% and Overhead % for snow becomes 15% + 100% = 115%. By hand, 115% x $60.00 = $69.00 of overhead gives the same $137.00 cost and $182.67 price. But 115% is not a normal overhead rate. It is that high only because a full season of truck and blade cost is loaded onto half the work, and the calculator's Overhead % field stops at 100%, so it will not accept it. The per-push line handles a thin winter without any of that.</p>
  <p>In a thin winter, the truck and blade cost more per push than the labor that runs them. You usually will not know in November whether the winter will be thin, so the practical move is to price on a conservative push count, then see whether that price is still one your lots will pay. If $182.67 per push is out of reach on a lot like this, the answer may be a longer recovery period, a cheaper setup, more lots on the route, or a seasonal contract for some customers, which is where the contract vs per-push post comes in.</p>

  <h3>A minimum charge for a tiny lot</h3>
  <p>Now say the route also includes a tiny lot: an assumed 0.25 hours on site, 1 worker, the same assumed $40.00 loaded rate, the same 0.25 hours of drive, the same $8.00 of fuel, and no salt. Go back to the normal 200-push winter.</p>
  <p>This is where the per-push line earns its place. Under the Overhead % alternative, 65% overhead on this stop's $20.00 base would carry only 50% x $20.00 = $10.00 of equipment, a third of the $30.00 share, even though the truck still drove there, dropped the blade, and used a full slot of its season. With the main method, the full $30.00 share rides on every push. Overhead % stays at the normal 15%, and Fuel / Travel Cost holds the fuel plus the full equipment share.</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th>Line</th><th>Arithmetic and result</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Labor</td><td>0.25 x 1 x $40.00 = $10.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Drive labor</td><td>0.25 x 1 x $40.00 = $10.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Materials at cost</td><td>$0.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Overhead</td><td>15% x ($10.00 + $10.00 + $0.00) = 15% x $20.00 = $3.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Fuel / Travel Cost</td><td>$8.00 fuel + $30.00 equipment share = $38.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Total cost</td><td>$10.00 + $10.00 + $0.00 + $3.00 + $38.00 = $61.00</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Floor price</td><td>$61.00 / 0.75 = $81.33, markup $0.00, so $81.33</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Profit</td><td>$81.33 minus $61.00 = $20.33</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Margin earned</td><td>$20.33 / $81.33 = 25.0%</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <p>In this example, the minimum charge per push is $81.33, and many shops would round that up to a clean figure. Compare it to the small lot in example 1. The tiny lot takes a third of the on-site time (0.25 hours against 0.75 hours), yet its floor is $81.33 against $142.67, about 57% of the small-lot price. That gap is the drive, the fuel, and the truck, which cost the same whether the lot is big or small. A per-push price scaled only by lot size would leave this stop losing money every storm.</p>

<section>
  <h2>Setting a floor for small lots</h2>
  <p>Once you have run the tiny-lot math for your own numbers, make the result a written rule: no push is quoted below the minimum. Put it on your rate sheet and in your estimates so customers see it before the first storm. The <a href="/blog/how-to-write-contractor-estimate.html">how to write a contractor estimate</a> guide covers how to word terms like this.</p>
  <p>A few points to keep the floor honest:</p>
  <ul>
    <li>Build it from the longest realistic drive on your route, not the shortest.</li>
    <li>Include the full equipment share per push, since a small stop uses a full slot of the truck's season.</li>
    <li>Rerun it whenever your push count, loaded rate, or setup cost changes.</li>
    <li>Apply it to driveways and pads too, if you take any residential work.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>The floor also protects you when a route is spread out. If a lot sits far from your other stops, the drive can take longer than the push. In that case the drive labor and fuel lines can be bigger than the on-site labor line, and the floor is what keeps that stop profitable.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Edge cases that break a push price</h2>
  <h3>A heavy storm that needs two pushes</h3>
  <p>Long storms often need a mid-storm push and a cleanup push. Each visit is a push, and each should be billed as one, as long as your terms say so up front. Count those extra visits in your season records, because they raise your real push count and lower the equipment share in later years. Do not count them in advance when you set the share, or a thin winter catches you out.</p>
  <h3>A warm winter with few pushes</h3>
  <p>Example 2 showed the effect: half the pushes, double the equipment share. The defense is pricing on a conservative count from the start and checking at mid-season. If you reach the middle of the season with far fewer pushes than planned, you will not recover the full year's equipment cost, and that shortfall should shape next season's price or count.</p>
  <h3>A blade breakdown mid-season</h3>
  <p>A broken blade costs you in two ways: the repair, and the pushes you cannot make while it is down. Repairs are part of what the truck and blade cost you, so add an allowance from your own repair history to the equipment cost you spread across your pushes. For the lost pushes, decide before the season whether you will rent, borrow, or subcontract, and price that backup into the plan rather than discovering its cost at two in the morning.</p>
  <h3>A lot with tight islands</h3>
  <p>Parking islands, light poles, and cart corrals slow a plow down and add hand work. Square footage is a poor guide for these lots. Time an actual push and put the real figure in Estimated Hours. If you priced the lot before you had plowed it, recheck after the first storm and adjust before the next invoice cycle, within the terms of your agreement.</p>
  <h3>A route where the drive is longer than the push</h3>
  <p>When a lot is short and far away, drive labor and fuel make up most of the cost. Enter the real Drive Time and fuel, and let the minimum charge do its job. If the floor price for that lot is more than the customer will pay, that lot may not belong on your route.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Sanity checks before you send the price</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>Multiply your equipment share per push by your expected pushes and seasons; it should come back to your setup cost.</li>
    <li>Check that the equipment is counted once: as the per-push line in Fuel / Travel Cost, or in Overhead % if you use the alternative, not in both.</li>
    <li>Confirm Hourly Labor Rate is your loaded cost per hour and not the rate you bill.</li>
    <li>Look at the Margin earned line; with Material Markup at 0, it should match your Desired Profit Margin.</li>
    <li>Compare the smallest lot's price to your written minimum and raise it if it falls short.</li>
  </ul>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Common mistakes and how to fix them</h2>
  <p>Leaving the truck out entirely is the biggest one. Shops often price labor and fuel and assume the equipment is already paid for. Even a paid-off truck has a replacement coming, so carry a share for the next one.</p>
  <p>Using the best winter on record as the push count is a close second. It makes the cost per push look low and the price look competitive, then leaves a gap in every normal year. Use your lower years.</p>
  <p>Billing rate in the labor field inflates cost before margin is applied, so the price runs high and the margin you think you earned is not what you earned. Enter the loaded cost and let margin do the rest.</p>
  <p>Scaling price only by lot size ignores the fixed cost of getting the truck to the lot. The minimum charge corrects it.</p>
  <p>Setting the price once and never checking it is the last one. Recount pushes at mid-season, compare against the count you priced on, and carry what you learn into next year's quote.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Run your own push</h2>
  <p>Gather your setup cost, your push count from records, your loaded labor rate, and a timed push on one real lot. Put the equipment share on each push, then enter it all in the <a href="/calculator">calculator</a> and set your minimum. Once the numbers come from your own shop instead of a guess, you will know whether each push pays for the truck that makes it.</p>
