<section>
  <p>Published ice dam removal cost figures center on hourly steam work. <a href="https://www.a-rite.com/knowledge-center/ice-dam-removal-cost-guide-2026">A-RITE Exteriors</a> in Central Wisconsin lists steam at about $400 to $600 per hour, with a typical residential job running 2 to 4 hours and generally landing between $800 and $2,400 (November 5, 2026), and <a href="https://www.angi.com/articles/ice-dam-removal-cost.htm">Angi's U.S. national cost guide</a> says most homeowners spend $650 to $2,400 (October 29, 2026). To price ice dam removal for your own crew, bill steam time by the hour from arrival and setup through pack-up, put roof snow on its own line when it has to come off first, and count the steamer, rented or owned, as a cost of the job.</p>
  <p>This guide walks through building that ticket in the free <a href="/calculator">JobProfitCalc calculator</a> from your own loaded labor cost, then checking the result against the published ranges. It is written for roofers, snow operations with roof capability, handymen, and general contractors who take ice dam calls and want a price that covers the crew, the machine, and the overhead behind both.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>What the published numbers actually tell you</h2>
  <p>Every figure below is a published range or one company's posted prices. Treat them as a check on your final price. Do not type a published rate into Hourly Labor Rate.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><a href="https://www.a-rite.com/knowledge-center/ice-dam-removal-cost-guide-2026">A-RITE Exteriors</a>, a roofing contractor serving Central Wisconsin from Schofield (Stevens Point north to Minocqua along Highway 51, including Wisconsin Rapids), published its pricing guide on November 5, 2026. It lists steam at about $400 to $600 per hour, a typical residential steam job of 2 to 4 hours at generally $800 to $2,400, and roof snow at about $250 to $600 per hour. A-RITE says two-story snow work costs more per hour, and that a 5/12 pitch or steeper, or a metal roof, adds to the hourly rate for both snow and steam. It publishes no dollar amount for those adders.</li>
    <li><a href="https://icedamremovalguys.com/charge/">Ice Dam Guys</a>, with a Minneapolis, MN address and service in many U.S. states and Canada, says on its rates page (modified February 12, 2026) that it typically charges $875 an hour for both roof snow removal and ice dam removal, with a 3-hour minimum. The same page says the company may charge more or less, or change the minimum, depending on region, travel, roof height or slope, extra technicians, how soon the work is needed, and special roof or gutter materials.</li>
    <li><a href="https://www.angi.com/articles/ice-dam-removal-cost.htm">Angi's U.S. national guide</a> (updated October 29, 2026) puts the national average for ice dam removal at $1,200, with most homeowners spending $650 to $2,400. Its method table lists steam at $800 to $2,400, manual removal (chiseling, chipping, or roof raking) at $800 to $1,800, chemical treatment at $200 to $400, and pressure washing at $400 to $2,000. It says most providers charge $150 to $700 per hour, commonly with a two-hour minimum, that a two-person crew is standard, and that snow removal runs $200 to $700.</li>
    <li><a href="https://homeguide.com/costs/ice-dam-removal-cost">HomeGuide's U.S. national guide</a> (modified October 17, 2025) lists steam at $800 to $2,400, or $400 to $600 per hour over 2 to 4 hours, and gives labor in general as typically $100 to $600 per hour. It puts raking a roof clear of snow at $200 to $500 per visit.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Two cautions before you lean on any of this. First, the Angi and HomeGuide method tables carry the same steam band, so read them as two published guides that agree. Do not treat the shared band as two independent surveys of the same jobs. Second, A-RITE's $400 to $600 per hour in Central Wisconsin and Ice Dam Guys' typical $875 per hour with a three-hour minimum describe different companies in different markets. Averaging them into one "going rate" produces a number nobody actually published.</p>
  <p>Angi (U.S. national, October 29, 2026) calls steam "the most popular professional method" for ice dams. A-RITE describes its own steam work as melting the ice with heat instead of breaking it apart with force, and HomeGuide's FAQ (U.S. national, October 17, 2025) says professionals run steamers at 290 degrees Fahrenheit through a low-pressure nozzle. Those are descriptions of method from those pages. For pricing purposes, the point is that steam work is slow, metered by the hour, and depends on a machine that has to be paid for.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Why one roof gets two lines on the ticket</h2>
  <p>Ice dam removal leads the ticket. It is the reason for the call, it is usually done with steam, and it is billed by the hour. A-RITE starts its clock at arrival and setup and stops it at pack-up, so the hour count includes rolling out hose, getting the machine running, and the minutes the wand is on the ice.</p>
  <p>Roof snow is a second line. A-RITE (Central Wisconsin, November 5, 2026) notes that deep snow sitting on the ice gets cleared first so steam can reach the dam. Sometimes that snow work is a small slice of the visit, and sometimes clearing the roof is the entire job with no steaming at all. Either way it has different hours, often no machine, and a different market band, so it deserves its own line.</p>
  <p>Keeping the lines apart does three things for you. The customer sees what each part of the visit costs. You can quote snow-only roofs without dragging the steamer cost into them. And when you look back at a season, you can see whether the steam line or the snow line is carrying the profit.</p>
  <p>Bill by the hour. None of the sources here publishes a per-foot rate for steam, and the hour is what both A-RITE and Ice Dam Guys actually meter.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>How to build the ice dam ticket, step by step</h2>
  <ol>
    <li>Start from your loaded labor cost per hour, meaning wages plus payroll taxes, workers' comp, and the other per-hour costs of having a tech on the clock. The <a href="/blog/burdened-labor-rate-for-contractors.html">burdened labor rate guide</a> covers how to build that number. This is your cost, and it goes in Hourly Labor Rate. A billed rate from a published guide never goes there.</li>
    <li>Estimate the hours from arrival to pack-up. Count setup, any roof-snow time, steam time, and pack-up. A-RITE (Central Wisconsin, November 5, 2026) and HomeGuide (U.S. national, October 17, 2025) both put a typical residential steam job at 2 to 4 hours, which is a reasonable sanity check for an average eave, though your own job history is the better guide.</li>
    <li>Choose the crew size. Angi (U.S. national, October 29, 2026) says a two-person crew is standard for this work. That is the guide's figure. Set Number of Workers to what you actually send.</li>
    <li>Put the steamer into the job cost. A rented machine goes into Material Cost as the yard's day rate plus whatever the yard charges for burner fuel and anti-freeze. An owned machine is covered further down.</li>
    <li>Add travel. Drive Time is the round-trip hours your crew spends in the truck, priced at the same loaded rate, and Fuel / Travel Cost is the truck's trip fuel only. Steamer fuel stays in Material Cost. The <a href="/blog/should-contractors-charge-for-drive-time.html">drive time post</a> explains why drive hours belong on the ticket.</li>
    <li>Set Overhead % from your real shop costs (the <a href="/blog/contractor-overhead-percentage.html">overhead percentage guide</a> shows how) and choose a Desired Profit Margin.</li>
    <li>Run the snow portion and the steam portion as two separate calculations so each has its own price, and confirm the two prices add up to the price for the whole visit.</li>
    <li>Compare the total against the published ranges for your area. If you land far outside them, check your inputs before you change your price.</li>
  </ol>
</section>

  <h3>Checking the example against the market</h3>
  <p>The $1,545.60 ticket sits inside Angi's most-homeowners band of $650 to $2,400 (U.S. national, October 29, 2026). If you sell snow-only roofs as standalone visits, that line will carry its own drive time and fuel, and its price will rise accordingly.</p>

<section>
  <h2>Short eaves, minimums, and the rental that does not shrink</h2>
  <p>Suppose a different call has a short eave with no snow to clear first. You can reuse the steam line from the example as-is: total cost $1,044.00, price $1,392.00, profit $348.00. Look at what drives that number. The $620.00 rental and fuel allowance is the single biggest cost, and it stays $620.00 whether the steaming takes two hours or forty minutes, because the yard rents by the day.</p>
  <p>This is why published minimums exist. Angi (U.S. national, October 29, 2026) notes that providers commonly require a two-hour minimum, and Ice Dam Guys (Minneapolis, February 12, 2026) publishes a three-hour minimum. Those are those pages' figures. Decide your own minimum from your own math: if a short job cannot cover a full rental day plus drive time and overhead at your margin, either set a minimum billed time or quote the job at the price the calculator gives for a realistic visit.</p>
  <p>When you can stack two or three ice dam jobs on the same rental day, the day rate spreads across them. In that case, split the rental between the tickets in Material Cost in proportion to the hours each job uses, and enter each job's own share instead of putting the full $600.00 on every ticket.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Edge cases that change the inputs</h2>
  <h3>After-hours and weekend calls</h3>
  <p>Ice dams often get reported when water shows up, which is rarely during business hours. For an after-hours visit, keep everything else the same and change only Hourly Labor Rate to your after-hours loaded cost. The <a href="/blog/after-hours-rate-for-contractors.html">after-hours rate guide</a> covers how to set that number, so there is no need to rebuild it here.</p>
  <h3>Two-story homes, steep pitches, and metal roofs</h3>
  <p>A-RITE (Central Wisconsin, November 5, 2026) says two-story snow work costs more per hour and that a 5/12 pitch or steeper, or a metal roof, adds to the hourly rate for both snow and steam. It does not publish a dollar figure for those adders. In your own pricing, those conditions show up as more hours, more crew, or both, so raise Estimated Hours or Number of Workers to reflect the slower work on that roof. Ice Dam Guys (Minneapolis, February 12, 2026) also lists roof height and slope among the reasons its own price may move.</p>
  <h3>An owned steamer</h3>
  <p>If you own the machine, it still costs you money on every job. Ice Dam Guys says each of its customized steamers cost "around eight-thousand dollars" (Minneapolis, February 12, 2026). That is that company's equipment cost. Carry your own machine one of two ways. You can put a per-job share in Material Cost, using your own assumed figure for what the machine costs you per season divided by the jobs you realistically expect. Or you can fold the machine into your shop overhead and let Overhead % pick it up, as the <a href="/blog/contractor-overhead-percentage.html">overhead percentage guide</a> describes. Pick one method and use it consistently so the steamer is never counted twice or not at all. Burner fuel and anti-freeze still go in Material Cost for each job.</p>
  <h3>Water already inside the house</h3>
  <p>If water is already coming through the ceiling and the roof needs to be covered, that is emergency tarp work with its own pricing, covered in the <a href="/blog/how-to-price-emergency-roof-tarp.html">emergency roof tarp guide</a>.</p>
  <h3>Extra work found mid-job</h3>
  <p>Damaged shingles, a torn gutter, or interior work the customer asks about while the crew is on site is new scope. Write it up as a change order instead of absorbing it into the steam hours; the <a href="/blog/contractor-change-order-scope-creep.html">change order guide</a> walks through it.</p>
  <h3>Prevention and heat cable</h3>
  <p>Heat-cable installation or other prevention work is a different job with its own materials and hours, so quote it separately from removal.</p>
  <h3>Site requirements</h3>
  <p>A-RITE (Central Wisconsin, November 5, 2026) notes its steam work needs an outdoor water spigot and a standard outdoor electrical outlet. Confirm both before the crew rolls. A missing spigot or a frozen hose bib costs crew time, and that time belongs in your hour estimate if you expect it.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Sanity checks before the quote goes out</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>Estimated Hours runs from arrival to pack-up and includes setup, any snow time, steam time, and pack-up.</li>
    <li>Hourly Labor Rate is a loaded cost per worker hour, well below any billed rate in the guides.</li>
    <li>The steamer appears once, either in Material Cost or in overhead, never in both and never missing.</li>
    <li>Steamer fuel and anti-freeze are in Material Cost, and only truck trip fuel is in Fuel / Travel Cost.</li>
    <li>Drive Time, trip fuel, and the steamer sit on the steam line when the ticket is split, and the snow line and steam line add back to the combined price.</li>
    <li>The final price falls somewhere sensible against the published ranges for your region and their dates. If it lands far below them, look for a missing cost before celebrating a low bid. If it lands far above them, check your hours and crew.</li>
    <li>Your margin matches what you aim for in this kind of work; the <a href="/blog/contractor-profit-margins-by-trade.html">profit margins by trade guide</a> gives context.</li>
  </ul>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Common mistakes and how to fix them</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>Pasting a billed hourly rate into Hourly Labor Rate. Typing a published hourly price from any of the guides above there treats someone else's selling price as your cost, and the calculator then adds overhead and margin on top of a number that already contains them. Enter your loaded cost and let the calculator build the price.</li>
    <li>Blending snow and steam into one lump. A single number hides which part of the visit makes money and forces you to haul steamer costs into snow-only jobs. Run two lines and show both on the ticket.</li>
    <li>Leaving the rental out because the machine "comes with the job." A rental day rate is a real cost, and on a short eave it can be the largest one. Put it in Material Cost.</li>
    <li>Putting steamer fuel in Fuel / Travel Cost. That field is added after overhead and is meant for truck fuel. Machine fuel and anti-freeze belong in Material Cost.</li>
    <li>Quoting per linear foot. None of the sources here publish a per-foot steam rate, and steam work is metered by time. Bill by the hour from setup to pack-up.</li>
    <li>Averaging published rates from different regions. A Central Wisconsin hourly band and a Minneapolis company's hourly rate are different data points; cite each with its region and date instead of merging them.</li>
    <li>Confusing markup with margin. A 25% margin and a 25% markup produce different prices; the <a href="/blog/contractor-markup-vs-margin.html">markup vs margin guide</a> shows the difference.</li>
  </ul>
</section>
