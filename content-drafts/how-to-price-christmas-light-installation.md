<section>
  <p>Published U.S. national ranges put labor-only Christmas light installation at $2 to $5 per linear foot of roofline when you hang the customer's own strands (<a href="https://zeemy.com/guides/holiday-lighting-pricing">Zeemy</a>, updated Sep 10, 2026; <a href="https://www.angi.com/articles/how-much-does-christmas-light-installation-cost.htm">Angi</a>, updated Oct 8, 2026). When you supply commercial-grade C9 LEDs and keep them after the season, Zeemy's FAQ lists $5 to $8 per foot. Angi reports a national average Christmas light installation cost of $444, with typical jobs between $220 and $684 across 2,242 verified projects (updated Oct 8, 2026).</p>
  <p>Those numbers tell you where the market sits. They do not tell you whether your crew makes money at that price. This guide walks through how much to charge for Christmas light installation when you run a lighting crew, landscaping company, gutter shop, or handyman business: per-foot pricing on customer lights versus your own, height and lift adders, packages, takedown and storage, a minimum job, and a short note on storefronts. Then it runs two worked examples through the <a href="/calculator">JobProfitCalc calculator</a> so you can compare your own floor price against the published range.</p>

<section>
  <h2>Why the per-foot numbers look so different</h2>
  <p>Shop owners comparing Christmas light installation prices online often see $2 a foot in one place and $12 in another and assume the market is chaotic. Most of the gap comes from what each figure includes. Zeemy and Angi agree that labor-only work on the customer's strands runs $2 to $5 per foot. HomeGuide's $5 to $12 per linear foot (dated Oct 1, 2025) is an installed figure that includes the lights. Zeemy's supplied-light range of $5 to $8 sits at the bottom of HomeGuide's installed range for the same reason: both price in the strands.</p>
  <p>The sources also agree in other places. Takedown runs $100 to $400 per job at both Zeemy and HomeGuide. The minimum job is $150 to $300 at both Zeemy and Angi. Full property packages run $750 to $5,000 at all three. When two or three sources land on the same range, you can lean on it with more confidence than a figure only one source reports.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Customer lights or yours: who carries the dead-strand risk</h2>
  <p>The first decision on every quote is whose lights go on the house. It changes the price, the risk, and how the job runs.</p>
  <h3>Labor only on the customer's strands</h3>
  <p>When the homeowner hands you a bin of lights from the garage, you are selling time and skill. That is why the published rate is lower: $2 to $5 per foot labor only, with Zeemy listing $3 as typical. The catch is that customer strands are an unknown. Some are mixed brands, some have different bulb spacing, and some fail halfway up the ladder. Every failed strand costs you time spent troubleshooting, climbing down, and rehanging.</p>
  <p>Put the dead-strand policy in writing before the crew arrives. Test the customer's strands on the ground first. State on the estimate that strands which fail testing or fail during the install are the customer's to replace, and give them two choices: you can supply a replacement at your cost plus the normal pricing method, or you hang what works and leave the gap. Bill troubleshooting time beyond a short check as added labor. Without that language, you absorb the risk on a job priced as if there were none.</p>
  <h3>Supplying commercial-grade LED and keeping the lights</h3>
  <p>When you supply the lights, you buy commercial-grade C9 LEDs, cut them to the house, and keep ownership. The customer rents the result each season. The published range rises to $5 to $8 per foot (Zeemy FAQ) because you now carry the strand cost, the dead-strand risk, the storage, and the work of tracking which run belongs to which house. Zeemy notes supplied strands last five to seven seasons, so the purchase cost has to be recovered across those seasons, not in year one alone.</p>
  <p>Supplied lights usually mean faster installs and fewer surprises, since you know every strand was tested when it went into storage. That reliability is part of what the higher price buys, and it is a fair point to make on the estimate.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Adders for second stories, steep pitch, and lifts</h2>
  <p>Height and pitch slow a crew down and raise the risk. Zeemy lists an adder of $1 to $4 per foot for second-story eaves, steep pitch, or lift work, and a separate premium and multi-story range of $7 to $12 per foot. Add the adder only to the feet that need it. A ranch house with one gable that climbs to a second story gets the adder on that gable run, not on the whole roofline.</p>
  <p>Lift days need their own line. If you rent a lift for a tall entry or a commercial facade, that rental is a direct job cost. Enter it in the calculator's Material Cost field at what you pay so overhead and margin apply to it, and add the lift hours to the job's Hours. Do not bury a lift rental inside a flat per-foot price that was built for ladder work.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Package pricing or per-foot pricing</h2>
  <p>Per-foot pricing fits rooflines. It scales cleanly, it is easy to explain, and a customer can see why a bigger house costs more. Package pricing fits the jobs homeowners actually ask for: "the front of the house," "roofline plus the two trees by the door," "the whole property." Zeemy lists front-of-house packages at $250 to $600 for one story and $400 to $1,000 for two story, based on about 100 feet of roofline. Full property packages run $750 to $5,000 across all three sources.</p>
  <p>Build a package from the parts, then round it into a clean number:</p>
  <ul>
    <li>Roofline feet at your per-foot rate, with the height adder on the feet that need it.</li>
    <li>Each extra item (a tree wrap, a wreath, a walkway run, window outlines) priced from the hours it takes on your crew.</li>
    <li>Takedown, and storage if you supply the lights.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Then check the package total against the published package range for that house size. If your two-story front package lands well above $1,000, look at the footage first. Zeemy's range assumes about 100 feet, so a house with far more roofline should cost more, and you can say so on the estimate.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Quote takedown and storage on the first estimate</h2>
  <p>Takedown is half the job, and it happens in January when nobody is excited about lights anymore. If it is not on the original estimate, it becomes an awkward second sale. Zeemy and HomeGuide both put takedown at $100 to $400 per job, and Zeemy notes it runs about half the install price when quoted alone. Off-season storage runs $50 to $200 per season (Zeemy).</p>
  <p>Put install, takedown, and storage on one estimate with a single total, or show them as separate lines that the customer accepts together. Either way, the customer agrees to the whole season in October. That locks in the takedown revenue and lets you schedule the crew for January before the calendar fills with other work. For a fuller guide to laying out the document itself, see <a href="/blog/how-to-write-contractor-estimate.html">how to write a contractor estimate</a>.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Set a minimum job so small calls pay for the trip</h2>
  <p>A homeowner who wants one wreath hung over the garage still costs you a truck roll, drive time, setup, and cleanup. Zeemy and Angi both list a minimum job of $150 to $300. Pick a minimum inside that range that covers your crew's time door to door, and apply it to small installs, takedown-only calls, and mid-season service visits. How to treat travel itself is covered in <a href="/blog/should-contractors-charge-for-drive-time.html">should contractors charge for drive time</a>.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>A short note on storefronts</h2>
  <p>Commercial Christmas light installation runs $6 to $12 per foot for storefronts (Zeemy, updated Sep 10, 2026). Businesses want lights up before their holiday traffic starts, so book them early, often before residential season fills up. Many storefronts can only be worked before opening or after closing, which changes your labor cost; <a href="/blog/after-hours-rate-for-contractors.html">after-hours rates for contractors</a> covers how to price that time. Multi-year terms are common, which suits shop-supplied lights because the strand cost can be spread over the contract seasons.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>How to price a Christmas light job, step by step</h2>
  <ol>
    <li>Measure the roofline. Walk the house with a tape or measuring wheel and record one-story and two-story feet separately. Add each extra item as its own line.</li>
    <li>Choose labor only or supplied lights. Decide whose strands go up, and write the dead-strand policy into the estimate if they are the customer's.</li>
    <li>Add height and access adders. Apply the adder to the second-story, steep, or lift feet only. Add lift rental as a cost.</li>
    <li>Add takedown and storage. Quote them now, on this estimate.</li>
    <li>Check the minimum. If the job total falls under your minimum, the minimum is the price.</li>
    <li>Run the job through the calculator. Enter hours, crew, loaded cost, materials, travel, overhead, and margin to get your floor price.</li>
    <li>Compare to the published range. Divide your price by the footage and see where it sits against the ranges above.</li>
  </ol>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>When your floor price lands above the market range</h2>
  <p>The calculator gives you a floor: the price that covers your costs and earns your target margin. If that floor sits above the published range, do not simply drop the price to match. Work through the causes in order.</p>
  <ul>
    <li>Start with hours. First-season installs on a new house run slow. Clips sized to the gutter, pre-cut runs, and a set ground routine cut hours more than anything else.</li>
    <li>Then look at crew size. Check whether a third worker is earning their hours or standing on the ground waiting.</li>
    <li>Check route density. Long drives between scattered houses inflate drive labor and fuel. Clustering jobs by neighborhood helps.</li>
    <li>Recheck the light cost per season. If you assumed fewer seasons than your strands really last, the per-season cost is too high. If you bought premium strands, the price should reflect it.</li>
    <li>Last, consider the job type. A floor above the labor-only range may be fine if the house qualifies for the height adder or the premium multi-story range.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>If the floor stays above the range after all that, the honest choices are to sell on reliability and service at your price, steer toward supplied-light packages where the range is higher, or pass on that job type. Pricing below your floor means paying to work. For the general logic of building a price up from costs, see <a href="/blog/how-to-price-a-contractor-job.html">how to price a contractor job</a>.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Edge cases that change the quote</h2>
  <h3>Customer strands that fail mid-install</h3>
  <p>Stop and tell the customer before buying replacements. Your written policy decides the next step: supply strands at cost plus your normal pricing, or hang what works. Track the troubleshooting time and bill it as added labor if it ran past the check you included.</p>
  <h3>Mixed one- and two-story rooflines</h3>
  <p>Measure each section separately and put the adder only on the high feet. A blended per-foot number hides the work and makes the next quote harder to repeat.</p>
  <h3>A lift day</h3>
  <p>Add the rental at cost in Material Cost and the lift hours in Hours. Zeemy's $1 to $4 adder covers lift work per foot, so compare your result with that adder applied.</p>
  <h3>Repeat customers in year two</h3>
  <p>Your strands are already cut and labeled, so the install takes fewer hours. Enter the real hours. You can keep the per-foot price steady and take the time savings as margin, or pass some of it on. That is a business choice you can make with the numbers in front of you.</p>
  <h3>A takedown-only call</h3>
  <p>Someone else hung the lights or the homeowner did, and now they want them down. Zeemy and HomeGuide put takedown at $100 to $400, and your minimum still applies. Price it from hours and travel, then hold the minimum if the result comes in under it.</p>
  <h3>Storm and mid-season service calls</h3>
  <p>Wind and snow knock runs loose. Treat these as their own trips priced at no less than your minimum job, which Zeemy lists at $150 to $300. Say on the original estimate whether service calls are included or billed separately.</p>
  <h3>Storefronts on multi-year terms</h3>
  <p>Spread the strand cost across the contract seasons, staying inside the five to seven seasons Zeemy cites for supplied strands. Build in when takedown happens and whether the business pays for off-hours work.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Common mistakes and how to fix them</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>The billing rate goes in as the labor rate. The calculator needs your loaded cost per hour. Entering a billing rate counts profit twice and pushes the price out of the market.</li>
    <li>New strands get charged to one season. Spread the purchase over the seasons you expect them to last. Otherwise year one looks overpriced and later years look like pure profit.</li>
    <li>Takedown is left off the estimate. Quote it in October. A January takedown sold after the fact is harder to collect and harder to schedule.</li>
    <li>A labor-only price gets compared with HomeGuide's installed range. HomeGuide's $5 to $12 includes lights. Compare labor-only work with the $2 to $5 range from Zeemy and Angi.</li>
    <li>The height adder goes on the whole house. Only the feet that need a second story, steep pitch, or lift get it.</li>
    <li>Small jobs skip the minimum. A one-wreath call priced from its hours alone, as in the example above, comes in under the published minimum and loses money on setup and travel.</li>
    <li>Fuel gets folded into the overhead base by hand. The calculator adds fuel after overhead. If you also folded it into overhead yourself, you would be charging overhead on fuel.</li>
  </ul>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Putting it together</h2>
  <p>Start every quote by measuring and deciding whose lights go up. Price customer strands near the $2 to $5 labor-only range and supplied commercial LEDs near Zeemy's $5 to $8, add the height adder where it belongs, and put takedown and storage on the same estimate. Hold a minimum for small calls. Then run the job through the <a href="/calculator">calculator</a> with your real hours and loaded cost, divide by the footage, and see where your floor sits. When it fits inside the published range, you can quote with confidence. When it does not, you know exactly which cost to work on.</p>
</section>
