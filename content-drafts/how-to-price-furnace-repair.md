<section>
  <p>A no-heat call at 11pm in February typically invoices $450 to $1,200 fully loaded, versus $250 to $500 for the same fix at 10am, according to <a href="https://pipelineon.com/blog/hvac-emergency-service-pricing/">PipelineOn</a>, a U.S. vendor research blog that cites other 2026 HVAC cost guides (published June 6, 2026). Treat those as published ranges rather than invoices or a rule for your shop. A small shop builds its own number from four pieces: a service call fee that states what the first hour includes, labor at the shop's loaded cost for that time of day, truck-stock parts entered at cost, and, when the part is not on the truck, a separate daytime return visit.</p>
  <p>This guide shows how to price furnace repair on that one ticket and how to run it through the free <a href="/calculator">job profit calculator</a>. It is written for the shop doing the pricing. Two worked examples close it out, and every input in them is an assumption you should replace with your own.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>How a no-heat call differs from a tune-up</h2>
  <p>A no-heat call starts with a failure the customer can already feel, so the ticket prices diagnosis and a fix on the customer's schedule, while a planned seasonal visit is priced the way the <a href="/blog/how-to-price-furnace-tune-up.html">furnace tune-up post</a> lays out.</p>
  <p>Cold nights also bring frozen and split lines on the plumbing side, and that sister ticket, with its access and drywall questions, is covered in the <a href="/blog/how-to-price-burst-pipe-repair.html">burst pipe repair post</a>.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>The ticket, step by step</h2>
  <ol>
    <li>Set the service call fee and write down exactly what it includes: diagnosis only, or diagnosis plus the first hour of labor.</li>
    <li>Choose the labor cost for the time of the call, using your daytime loaded cost or your after-hours loaded cost.</li>
    <li>Estimate the hours on site for the likely repair, and label any guess as a guess until you have your own job history.</li>
    <li>Enter truck-stock parts at what they cost your shop.</li>
    <li>Add Drive Time and the fuel for this trip.</li>
    <li>Run the calculator with your overhead percentage and target margin, then quote the fee as its own line next to the result.</li>
    <li>Decide whether this visit finishes the job or needs a daytime return for an ordered part, and price that return as its own ticket.</li>
    <li>Compare the quoted total to the published ranges and investigate any big gap before you send it.</li>
  </ol>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>The service call and the first hour of labor</h2>
  <p>Every no-heat call starts with a fee that gets a truck to the door and a technician in front of the furnace. Shops name it differently (service call, dispatch fee, diagnostic fee) and structure it differently, as the table above shows. Whether you credit that fee toward the repair or waive it is a policy question with its own trade-offs, and the <a href="/blog/should-contractors-waive-diagnostic-fee.html">diagnostic fee post</a> works through it.</p>
  <p>For pricing the ticket, the question that matters is narrower: what does your fee buy? If it covers diagnosis only, in the FortHouse style, then all repair labor still needs to be priced, and the calculator's Estimated Hours should include the full repair time. If it covers diagnosis and the first hour, in the All Island style, then the fee is already the billed price of that first hour. Running a full labor-and-margin price through the calculator for the same hour and adding the fee on top would charge the customer twice for it. In that structure, parts get added after the customer approves them in writing, and the calculator only needs to price any labor past the included hour plus the parts themselves.</p>
  <p>Either way, show the fee as its own quoted line and keep it outside the calculator math. The calculator prices the work; the fee is a separate line the customer agreed to when they booked. Keeping them apart also makes it obvious later which part of the ticket paid for the visit and which part paid for the repair.</p>
  <p>Drive time is the other piece of getting to the door. Whether you bill it as its own line is covered in the <a href="/blog/should-contractors-charge-for-drive-time.html">drive time post</a>. For this ticket, enter the trip in Drive Time so the cost of the technician sitting in the truck is counted whether or not it shows on the invoice.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>If the call is after hours, change the labor cost</h2>
  <p>A technician on a call at night or on a weekend usually costs the shop more per hour than the same person at 10am, through overtime, on-call pay, or both. That higher cost belongs in one place: the Hourly Labor Rate field. Enter your after-hours loaded cost there and leave every other input working the way it does during the day.</p>
  <p>How you get from a daytime loaded cost to an after-hours loaded cost is a separate decision, and the <a href="/blog/after-hours-rate-for-contractors.html">after-hours rate post</a> covers the method. If you have not built the daytime number yet, start with the <a href="/blog/burdened-labor-rate-for-contractors.html">burdened labor rate post</a>, which turns a wage into the full hourly cost of keeping a technician on the payroll.</p>
  <p>Raising the labor cost also raises the drive labor and the overhead, because both are calculated from it. That is the intended effect. A night drive costs more than a day drive for the same reason the night repair does.</p>
  <p>The after-hours dispatch fee is a different thing from the after-hours labor cost. PipelineOn's 2026 dispatch range of $150 to $500 (U.S. general, published June 6, 2026) describes what shops bill to roll a truck at night. Gale Force's after-hours add-on of $150 to $300 (Denver metro averages, not a quote, last updated October 26, 2026) is the same idea in one market. Neither is a labor input.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Parts on the truck go in as Material Cost</h2>
  <p>The failures that most often leave a furnace cold at night are small parts a well-stocked truck already carries. Gale Force names ignitors, flame sensors, and capacitors as common nighttime failures that are often fixed in one to two hours (Denver metro averages, not a quote, last updated October 26, 2026). FortHouse says four parts ride on every truck (ignitor, flame sensor, pressure switch, inducer motor), so most of those repairs finish on the same visit (Chicago's western suburbs, updated October 30, 2026).</p>
  <p>Enter whatever comes off the truck in Material Cost at what the part cost your shop. Not the price you would list on an invoice and not a retail figure from a supply house website. If the shop restocks from a supplier on account, use the last invoice cost for that part. If one part goes in and a second gets tested and kept, only the part that stays in the furnace belongs on this ticket.</p>
  <p>That one-to-two-hour figure from Gale Force is the only published hour count in this guide. Any other repair time, including the hours used in the examples below, is an assumption. Your own completed tickets are a better guide than any published average once you have a season of them.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>If the part is not on the truck, the return is a daytime visit</h2>
  <p>Some no-heat calls end with a diagnosis and a part that is not on the truck: a control board, a blower motor, a model-specific component. At night the supply house is closed. The first job at that point is to leave the system safe, following your shop's shutdown and documentation procedures, and to tell the customer in writing what was found and what is on order.</p>
  <p>Price the return as its own daytime visit. All Island Comfort says a customer should not pay a second emergency fee because the supply house was closed, and that the return is billed at daytime rates (Wading River, Long Island, NY, updated September 7, 2026). Pricing it that way also keeps the math honest, because the return really is a daytime trip: daytime loaded labor, daytime drive, and a part bought at normal supplier cost.</p>
  <p>If the shop provides temporary heat while the part is on order, list it as its own line on the first ticket or the return ticket, with its own price set by the shop. This guide does not suggest a figure for it, and no source above publishes one.</p>
  <p>The first visit then closes as a diagnosis-and-make-safe ticket: the service call fee, plus any labor past what the fee includes. The second visit is the repair, and it is the second worked example below.</p>
</section>

Say the swap takes 1.5 hours, an example that sits inside Gale Force's one to two hours for common nighttime failures. Say the shop's daytime loaded cost is $48.00 per hour and it applied 1.5x for after-hours work as an example choice, giving an assumed $72.00 after-hours loaded cost; the method for choosing that figure belongs to the <a href="/blog/after-hours-rate-for-contractors.html">after-hours rate post</a>. <section>
  <h2>Edge cases that change the ticket</h2>
  <h3>Diagnosis only, no repair</h3>
  <p>Sometimes the customer declines the repair, or the fix needs a decision from a landlord or a second opinion. The ticket is then the service call fee and nothing else, as long as the time on site stayed inside what the fee covers. If the diagnosis ran long, price the extra time through the calculator with zero materials and add it as a line, so the fee is not quietly absorbing labor it was never meant to pay for.</p>
  <h3>Two technicians on the call</h3>
  <p>Some shops send two people at night for safety, or pair a lead with a trainee. Set Number of Workers to 2. The calculator multiplies both labor and Drive Time by the crew size, so both people are counted on site and in the truck. Leaving the count at 1 on a two-person call understates labor and drive labor by half each, and the overhead and price shrink with them.</p>
  <h3>A one-hour minimum</h3>
  <p>If the shop bills a one-hour minimum, enter at least 1.0 in Estimated Hours even when a flame sensor cleaning takes less. When the fee already includes that first hour, the minimum is satisfied by the fee and the calculator only needs the time past it.</p>
  <h3>Overtime</h3>
  <p>Overtime is already part of the after-hours loaded cost in Hourly Labor Rate. Do not add it a second time as an extra line or by bumping Estimated Hours to account for the premium. The hours field should hold real hours; the rate field holds what those hours cost.</p>
  <h3>A ticket above or below the published ranges</h3>
  <p>When the quoted total lands outside the ranges in this guide, look for a reason before you change the price. A ticket well above PipelineOn's range for a night call can mean a long drive, a two-person crew, or an expensive part, all of which are legitimate. A ticket well below can mean the labor cost entered was too low, a part was entered at less than true cost, or the fee was left off entirely. The published ranges come from specific markets and dates, so a shop in a lower-cost or higher-cost area may sit outside them for good reason.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Common mistakes and how to fix them</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>Typing a published hourly labor figure, such as a number from Gale Force's Denver range, into Hourly Labor Rate. Enter your loaded cost there instead, and use the published range only to check the finished price.</li>
    <li>Pricing a full first hour through the calculator when the service call fee already includes it. Remove that hour from Estimated Hours so the customer pays for it once.</li>
    <li>Folding the dispatch fee into Material Cost or Fuel / Travel Cost to make it show up in the total. Overhead and margin would then apply to it. Quote it as its own line.</li>
    <li>Charging a second emergency fee on the return trip for an ordered part. Price the return as a daytime visit with daytime labor cost.</li>
    <li>Entering a part at an installed price from a published range. FortHouse's ignitor range, for example, is a finished repair price.</li>
    <li>Comparing your quote to FortHouse's repair figures without adding its $119 diagnostic, or to All Island's fees without remembering they include the first hour.</li>
    <li>Forgetting Drive Time on a short trip because it feels small. Half an hour at an after-hours cost adds up across a winter of night calls.</li>
    <li>Inventing a price for temporary heat on the fly. Set it once as a shop line item so every technician quotes the same thing.</li>
  </ul>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Putting the no-heat call together</h2>
  <p>A clean no-heat ticket has the same shape whether the call comes in at noon or midnight: a fee that says what it covers, labor at the right loaded cost for the hour, parts at cost, drive and fuel counted, and a margin applied to the whole. Night work changes one input, the labor cost, and adds a dispatch fee as its own line. A missing part splits the job into two tickets, with the second priced as a daytime visit. Run the numbers in the <a href="/calculator">calculator</a>, check the result against the published ranges from the market closest to yours, and quote emergency furnace repair with the fee and the repair on separate lines so the customer can see both.</p>
