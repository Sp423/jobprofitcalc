How to Put Diesel Fuel Cost Into Every Job Price

October 20, 2026 · 13 min read · JobProfitCalc.com

To price diesel into a job, estimate the gallons the job will burn, multiply by a current price per gallon, and enter that dollar figure in the Fuel / Travel Cost field of the job profit calculator. Then show it as its own fuel line on the estimate. Do that on every job and your contractor fuel cost stops coming out of profit.

The reason to do it now is the size of the number. The EIA weekly retail series put U.S. on-highway diesel at $6.179 per gallon for the week of Oct 12, 2026 (national average, all taxes included, released Oct 14, 2026). The same series read $3.665 for the week of Oct 13, 2025.

The year-over-year change is ($6.179 - $3.665) / $3.665 = 68.6%.

The last few weeks drifted down, from $6.529 (week of Sep 21, 2026) to $6.199 (week of Oct 5, 2026) and $6.179 (week of Oct 12, 2026) in the same EIA series, then turned back up to $6.276 for the week of Oct 19, 2026, a 9.7 cent rise in one week (EIA U.S. on-highway diesel, released Oct 20, 2026). A gallon still costs far more than the number most shops carried in their heads a year ago, and a fuel guess that was close enough last fall is now badly short.

Shops are already feeling it in their budgets. Spezio Property Services, a Rochester, NY snow and plow company, told Rochester First (WROC) on Sep 21, 2026 that it had budgeted about $30,000 to $35,000 for fuel this season against a typical $15,000, with diesel around $6.37 a gallon in Rochester (Rochester First). An annual budget like that only gets recovered one job at a time, which is what the rest of this post walks through.

The whole method in five steps

  1. Count the gallons the job will burn. Use road miles, machine run time, or your own fill history, whichever fits the job. Most diesel-heavy jobs need two of the three added together.
  2. Pick one price per gallon and write down where it came from and the date. Your fuel card, your local pump, or the EIA weekly number all work if you use them consistently.
  3. Multiply gallons by price. The result is the job's fuel cost in dollars.
  4. Enter that figure in the Fuel / Travel Cost field in the calculator, alongside your usual hours, workers, labor rate, materials, overhead, and margin.
  5. Show fuel as a named line on the estimate, and set a short quote valid window while prices are moving.

If you are new to building a price from cost, the full sequence is in how to price a contractor job. This post stays on the fuel piece.

Three ways to count the gallons

Road miles divided by your truck's real mpg

Take the round-trip miles from the shop (or wherever the truck starts) to the site, multiply by the number of trips, and divide by the truck's miles per gallon. A job with an assumed 40 round-trip miles and three trips is 120 miles. At an assumed 10 mpg, that is 12 gallons.

Round-trip miles come from a GPS or telematics trip report, a map route, or two odometer readings. The trip count comes from your dispatch board or schedule, and it should include supply runs, dump runs, and the trip to drop and pick up equipment.

The mpg has to be your truck's, measured the way the truck will be driven on this job. Fill the tank, note the odometer, drive a normal week, fill again, and divide miles driven by gallons added. If you have never measured it, do that this week and use a deliberately low assumed mpg until you have the real figure. A low mpg overstates gallons slightly, which is the safe direction for a quote.

Machine hours times gallons per hour from your fill log

For skid steers, excavators, chippers, generators, and anything else that burns diesel on site, multiply the hours the machine will run by its gallons per hour. The gallons per hour should come from your own fill log. Divide the gallons you put in over a period by the hour-meter hours over the same period.

Use hour-meter hours, since the meter counts idling. A machine that idles half the day burns less per hour than one that digs all day, and your log average already blends the two the way your crew actually runs it. If you have no fill log, start one now with three columns (date, gallons added, hour-meter reading). Until you have a month of entries, fill the machine before a job and again after it, and use that job's gallons as your first data point.

Fill-ups or fuel card history for a job you repeat

For a route, a recurring maintenance stop, or a job type you do every week, skip the estimating and use history. Pull the fuel card statement for a stretch of days when the truck and machines did only that kind of work, total the gallons, and divide by the number of jobs. That is your gallons per job, including idling, traffic, and detours.

This method only works when the fuel on the statement belongs to those jobs. If the same truck also ran estimates and personal errands that week, the average is polluted, and you are better off with miles and hours.

Which price per gallon to multiply by

You have three reasonable choices, and they will not match each other.

  • Your fuel card average is what you actually paid, discounts included. It is the most accurate for your shop, but it lags. A statement covering the last 30 days reflects old prices in a rising market.
  • Your local pump price today is current and local. It takes a minute to check and is the best choice for a job starting this week.
  • The EIA weekly number is a national average published on a schedule, which makes it the best public reference to write on a quote. It is a national figure, so your pump may sit above or below it.

The Rochester report shows how far local and national prices can sit apart. Rochester First put diesel around $6.37 in Rochester on Sep 21, 2026, while the EIA national average for the week of Sep 21, 2026 was $6.529. Pick one source, use it on every quote, and note the date.

Gasoline vans need a gasoline price. Run the same math with the van's measured mpg and what you pay for gas, and do not borrow the diesel figure. A shop with both diesel trucks and gas vans should compute each vehicle's gallons at its own fuel price and add the dollars.

If you run dyed off-road diesel in machines, price those gallons at what you actually pay for that fuel. The EIA on-highway figure includes all taxes, so it may not match your off-road price. Check federal and state rules on where dyed fuel can be used before you rely on it.

Where fuel sits in the calculator math

In this calculator, fuel is not part of the overhead base. Overhead is applied to labor, drive labor, and materials at cost, and fuel is added after overhead. Your desired margin then applies to the whole total cost, fuel included. A fuel line entered at cost still earns your margin, so there is no need to pad the gallons or the price.

Keep the fuel line to fuel. Truck and equipment payments, insurance, and maintenance are ownership costs that you pay whether or not the truck moves, and they belong in your overhead percentage. The method for building that number is in contractor overhead percentage. If you load ownership costs into the fuel line too, you charge for them twice.

Drive time is a separate cost. The hours your crew spends on the road are paid labor, and they go in the Drive Time field as hours, where the calculator multiplies them by crew size and your labor rate. Fuel is the diesel burned during those hours and goes in as dollars. A job needs both lines, and neither one covers the other. How to decide what to charge for those hours is covered in should contractors charge for drive time.

When a named fuel line beats burying diesel in margin

Some shops skip the fuel line and raise the margin to cover diesel. On the example job, that would mean quoting the $2,812.80 no-fuel cost at a price of $3,917.64, which takes a margin of ($3,917.64 - $2,812.80) / $3,917.64 = about 28.2%. That figure is only right for this job. A service call that burns 2 gallons and a grading job that burns 52 need very different bumps, so one blended margin overcharges the light jobs and undercharges the heavy ones.

A separate fuel line is the better choice when any of these is true:

  • Fuel is a meaningful share of the ticket, as it is on hauling, excavation, plowing, mowing routes, and long-distance jobs.
  • Gallons vary a lot from job to job.
  • The price per gallon is moving week to week, as the recent EIA readings show.
  • You want customers and your own crew to see why the price changed, and to see it drop when diesel drops.

The jump shows up upstream too. AGC's analysis of BLS producer price data put diesel fuel up 77.8% from August 2025 to August 2026, measured at the refinery and terminal level rather than the pump (AGC, Sep 10, 2026). A visible line tied to a public number is easier to defend than a margin that quietly grew.

Setting up a simple fuel surcharge

  1. Write the base price per gallon on the quote, with its source and date. For example, a quote could state a base of $6.179 per gallon, EIA national on-highway diesel, week of Oct 12, 2026 (released Oct 14, 2026), the latest week published when this post was written.
  2. Write the estimated gallons the fuel line is built on. In the example job, that is 52.
  3. Name the reference you will check when the work is performed. Use the EIA weekly series if you want a number the customer can look up, or your fuel card statement if you want your actual cost.
  4. State the adjustment in one sentence: estimated gallons times the difference between the reference price in the week of the work and the base price, up or down.
  5. Set a short quote valid window so an unsigned quote does not sit for two months at an old base. How to choose that window is covered in how long a contractor quote is good for.

Here is the adjustment with assumed numbers. If the reference price in the week of the work were an assumed $6.50, the adjustment on the example job would be 52 x ($6.50 - $6.179) = 52 x $0.321 = $16.69 added.

If the reference were an assumed $5.90, it would be 52 x ($6.179 - $5.90) = 52 x $0.279 = $14.51 credited back.

The credit matters as much as the charge. A surcharge that only ever goes up reads as a price increase, and customers treat it that way.

On the estimate itself, the fuel line should sit with the other cost lines and carry its basis in plain words (gallons, base price, source, date). Layout and wording for the rest of the document are in how to write a contractor estimate. For seasonal agreements that need formal pass-through wording, the clause language is in snow removal contract vs per push.

Jobs where the simple math needs a second look

A job far outside your usual radius

Miles scale directly, so run the real round trip times the real trip count. Then decide whether equipment stays on site or comes home each night, because that choice can double the towing miles. Check the pump price near the job as well if you will be fueling there.

Equipment on a trailer

A truck towing a loaded trailer gets worse mileage than the same truck empty. Measure mpg across a tank that was mostly towing and keep it as a second number. Use the towing mpg for hauling trips and the empty mpg for the trips without the trailer.

Machines and trucks that idle

A fill log divided by hour-meter hours already includes idle burn. Truck idling is the part that gets missed. A truck that runs for heat, a PTO, or warning lights while parked burns fuel that road miles will never show. For those jobs, the fill-up method is more honest than miles divided by mpg.

A multi-day job where diesel moves mid-job

For a job of a week or less, price at today's number and accept the small drift. For longer jobs, use the surcharge setup above so the fuel line follows the reference price. A fixed fuel line with a short quote valid window is the other option.

Jobs already signed at an old fuel price

If the signed agreement has no fuel adjustment language, the price stands. Compute the gap the way the example does (gallons times the difference in price) so you know what the job is costing you, and fix it on the next quote or at renewal. Adding a surprise charge to a signed job can cost you the customer over a few dollars of fuel.

Small service calls

On a short call, fuel is small and per-job math is overkill. Say a service truck makes an assumed 24-mile round trip at an assumed 12 mpg. That is 2 gallons, and 2 x $6.179 = $12.36.

Work that out once for each service zone, enter it as a flat Fuel / Travel Cost figure for calls in that zone, and revisit the figure monthly. That is small on one call and real money over a year of calls.

Sanity checks and common mistakes

Run two checks. First, divide the fuel line by the job price. The example landed at about 8.2% for a machine-heavy job. If a light service call shows a share like that, or a hauling job shows almost nothing, recheck the gallons. Second, at month end, add up the fuel lines on every job you billed and compare the total to your fuel card statement for the same month. If the statement is higher, some gallons are not making it into quotes.

The mistakes that cause that gap are usually these:

  • Shops count one-way miles. Always use the round trip, and count every trip.
  • Shops price the truck and forget the machine. Add equipment gallons from run hours on any job with diesel equipment on site.
  • A brochure or dashboard mpg stands in for the real one. Measure it between fill-ups, loaded the way you work.
  • The per-gallon number comes from memory. Check the pump, the card, or the EIA release before each quoting session and write the date next to it.
  • Truck payments or repairs end up in the fuel line. Those belong in overhead.
  • The drive-time line gets treated as covering fuel. Hours and gallons are two separate costs and both get entered.
  • Shops pad the fuel line to "make something on it." Margin already applies to the total cost, so enter fuel at cost.

Once the gallons and the price are in hand, the entry takes seconds. Open the calculator, put the dollar figure in Fuel / Travel Cost, and compare the price to the one you would have quoted without it.
