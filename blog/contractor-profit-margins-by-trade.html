Contractor Profit Margins by Trade: What's Normal in 2026?

June 9, 2026 · Updated October 4, 2026 · 9 min read · JobProfitCalc.com

Published US data put average contractor net margins at about 4% to 9% with owner pay counted as a cost (CFMA, NAHB, ACCA, IRS); owner-operators who leave their own pay in profit see about 13% to 16% (IRS sole proprietors).

The number that matters is what share of every revenue dollar you keep after paying for labor, materials, overhead, and a fair wage for your own time on the tools. That is net margin, measured before income tax. Most trade contractors never sit down and calculate what they're actually keeping, or compare it to what healthy trade businesses look like.

Gross Margin vs. Net Margin: Know the Difference

Gross profit margin measures what's left after subtracting direct job costs, primarily labor and materials, from your revenue. It does not account for overhead: your truck payment, insurance, licensing, accounting, software, or any other cost that exists whether or not a specific job is running.

Gross Margin = (Revenue − Direct Job Costs) ÷ Revenue × 100

Net profit margin measures what's left after subtracting every cost of running the business: direct job costs plus all overhead, including pay for the owner's own work. The sources in this post report it before income tax. It is the money the business earned after it was fully paid.

Net Margin = (Revenue − All Costs) ÷ Revenue × 100

The gap between the two is large. In CFMA's 2026 benchmark, specialty trade contractors averaged 22.1% gross and 7.5% net (FY2025, US). NAHB found remodelers averaged 29.9% gross and 6.3% net (FY2024, US), and home builders 20.7% gross and 8.7% net (FY2023, US). Sources for all three are in the table below.

For example, a roofing contractor with a 35% gross margin sounds healthy. But if overhead (trucks, insurance, employees, yard space, equipment) consumes 20 percentage points of that, the net margin is 15%. If overhead is eating 28 points, net margin is 7%.

The benchmark numbers in this post are net margins. The sourced US averages, with owner pay counted as a cost, run about 4% to 9% net. A gross margin in the 20s or 30s is not the same thing and shouldn't be compared to them.

Service Work vs. New Construction: Why Contractors Price Them Differently

No published data measures a margin gap between service and new construction work. What follows are the reasons many contractors price service work higher per hour. Treat them as reasons to check your own job costs, not as a measured difference.

  • Urgency pricing. A homeowner whose furnace failed at 6 a.m. in January is not comparison shopping. They are calling whoever picks up the phone. Urgency commands premium pricing that new construction bidding does not allow.
  • Smaller ticket, less price sensitivity. For example, a $350 service call doesn't trigger the same scrutiny as a $35,000 roof replacement, and a $400 plumbing repair is less likely to be shopped than a $12,000 bathroom remodel. For scale, the average HVAC service ticket in the ACCA study was $422 (Dec 5, 2025, US).
  • Diagnostic expertise. Finding and diagnosing a problem is skilled, billable work. Service technicians are paid for knowledge and problem-solving, not just labor. That expertise is worth pricing, not giving away.
  • Less competition per job. New construction and large replacement projects are formally bid in many markets. Service and repair calls are less likely to involve competitive bidding, which protects margin.

New construction is competitively bid, material-intensive, weather-dependent, and subject to delays outside your control. Each of those squeezes margin even on a well-priced job.

What's Happening to Margins in 2026

There is no 2026 margin data yet. The latest figures show margins holding roughly steady near recent highs. CFMA's all-firm net margin was 6.8% in FY2025 against 6.7% in FY2024, and specialty trades were 7.5% against 7.7% (CFMA 2026). NAHB's remodelers posted their highest net margin since 1996 in 2024, and builders' margins improved in 2023.

Steady industry averages don't protect your shop. If your labor, insurance, or material costs went up and your prices didn't, your margin went down. Check each against last year's invoices and policies, and reprice your overhead when it changes. We suggest reviewing prices at least once a year.

When customers are getting more quotes, a well-written, professionally presented estimate helps you hold your price.

The contractors who protect their margins are the ones who raise prices to match their actual cost increases, not the ones who absorb the increases and hope volume will compensate.

Is 20% Net Profit Realistic for a Solo Contractor?

It depends on whether the 20% includes your own pay.

Before paying yourself: sole proprietors in the specialty trades kept an average 15.7% of receipts (IRS, tax year 2023). Many owner-operators aim for 20% on that basis. That is a goal some reach, not the norm.

After paying yourself a market wage: the averages are 4% to 9%, and top performers reach the low-to-mid teens: 12.5% for CFMA's best-in-class firms overall, 14.1% for best-in-class specialty trades, and 17.7% for NAHB's top quarter of builders. A 20% net margin on this basis is well above what any source in this post reports as typical.

Set your own target from your overhead and goals. In the calculator, overhead is its own field, and your loaded labor rate should include a wage for your own hours. So Desired Profit Margin is what you want left after both. Work it out from the year:

  1. Estimate next year's revenue.
  2. Decide how much profit you want on top of your wage: for reserves, equipment, debt, and growth.
  3. Divide that profit by the revenue. That is your margin target.

Example: on $150,000 in annual revenue, a 20% net margin is $30,000 in profit before income tax. At 10% it's $15,000. At 30% it's $45,000. If the $15,000 you actually need for reserves and equipment works out to 10%, enter 10, not a number from someone else's business.

Warning Signs Your Margin Is Too Thin

These patterns indicate your margins have slipped below sustainable levels, often before the bank account confirms it.

  • You're always busy but never ahead: a full schedule and good revenue, but money is consistently tight. Revenue covers costs but not profit. The business sustains itself but doesn't build.
  • You dread looking at what jobs actually cost. If you don't track what you actually spent on labor and materials versus what you estimated, you cannot know your real margin. Most contractors who avoid this calculation are avoiding it because the number is uncomfortable.
  • Your pricing hasn't changed in over a year. If your costs have gone up, a rate that was profitable in 2024 may be break-even now. Margins erode silently when pricing stays flat and costs rise. We suggest reviewing prices at least once a year.
  • You cut prices to win jobs. If your default response to losing a bid is to lower your price, you're competing on cost rather than value. Contractors who cut margin to win work train customers to negotiate on price, and train themselves to price for volume rather than profitability.
  • You struggle to pay yourself consistently. If the business can't reliably support a consistent owner draw, the margin is too thin. The business should pay for your labor first (your hourly rate, built into job costs), and then generate profit on top of that. Inconsistent self-pay usually means the two have been conflated.

How to Calculate Your Own Margin

You don't need accounting software to get a reliable estimate of your current margin. The Job Profit Calculator at JobProfitCalc.com shows net profit margin at the job level, after labor, drive labor, materials at cost, overhead, and fuel. It also estimates a self-employment tax set-aside on the profit, which is not added to the price and doesn't include income tax.

For a big-picture view of your annual margin:

  1. Take your total revenue for the past 12 months
  2. Subtract all direct job costs (what you paid for labor and materials on jobs)
  3. Subtract all overhead costs (insurance, truck, tools, software, licenses, accounting)
  4. Subtract a market wage for your own hours, if you didn't already pay yourself one through payroll
  5. Divide what remains by total revenue

The result is your net margin before income tax, measured the way the first table above measures it. If you skip step 4, compare against the second table instead.

For what you'll actually take home, set taxes aside from that profit. Self-employment tax alone is about 14.1% of profit (15.3% × 92.35%, per the IRS). Add your federal and state income-tax rate on top.

Three Levers for Improving Your Margin

1. Raise Prices Before You Cut Costs

Most contractors reach for cost reduction when margins are thin. That is usually the wrong move first, because overhead cuts often mean cutting things the business actually needs. For example, a job that costs $900 and sells for $1,000 carries a 10.0% margin. Raise the price 5% to $1,050 with costs unchanged and the margin becomes 14.3%. Calculate what a $10 per hour rate increase does to your annual profit before you start cutting line items.

2. Make Sure Materials Carry Profit

Every job where materials go out at cost with no profit on them is a job where you're doing purchasing, carrying, and installation work for free. In the calculator, enter materials at cost; your margin covers profit on them, so there's no separate markup step. If you price materials by markup elsewhere, published guidance runs from about 10% to 20% on competitive bids (Mike Holt forum electricians) to 20% to 50% or more on smaller service jobs (HomeGuide, Housecall Pro). The post on how to mark up materials as a contractor covers the sources and the markup-to-margin math.

3. Protect Scope with Written Estimates and Change Orders

Scope creep, doing work beyond the original estimate without charging for it, is one of the most reliable margin killers in the trades. Every hour of uncompensated extra work reduces the effective margin on that job. A written estimate with clear scope and a stated change order policy is the only structural protection against it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What profit margin should a contractor make?

Published US averages run about 4% to 9% net with owner pay counted as a cost: 7.5% for specialty trades (CFMA, FY2025), 6% for HVAC (ACCA, 2025 study), 6.3% for remodelers (NAHB, 2024 data), 8.7% for builders (NAHB, 2023 data), and 4.8% to 6.0% for corporations by trade (IRS, tax year 2022). Top performers reach about 12.5% to 17.7%. Set your own target from your overhead and the profit you need on top of your wage.

Is 20% profit margin realistic for a contractor?

Only if the 20% includes your own pay. Before paying themselves, sole proprietors in the specialty trades kept an average 15.7% of receipts (IRS, tax year 2023). After a market wage for the owner, top performers reach the low-to-mid teens (CFMA best-in-class specialty trades 14.1%; NAHB top-quarter builders 17.7%). Many owner-operators aim for 20% before paying themselves; treat that as a goal, not a norm.

Do HVAC and plumbing contractors earn higher margins than drywall?

Not in the published data. Canadian government figures put drywall and insulation firms at 15.9% against 11.2% for plumbing and HVAC (ISED, 2024). US corporate data put plumbing and HVAC at 4.8% against 5.5% for other specialty trades (IRS, tax year 2022). Service pricing on HVAC, plumbing, and electrical calls can earn more per hour on a given job, but that doesn't show up as a higher trade-wide margin.

How do I know if I'm leaving money on the table?

Measure your net margin the same way a source in this post does, and compare it to that row. If you're well below it, look at your pricing and your overhead. Use the Job Profit Calculator on your next job and compare the Suggested Charge to what you would have quoted without it. The gap is your answer.

What's the difference between markup and margin?

Markup is calculated on cost; margin is calculated on revenue. A 25% markup means you added 25% to your cost to get your price. A 25% margin means 25% of your revenue is profit. These are different numbers: a 25% markup produces a 20% margin. Using markup and margin interchangeably leads to systematically underpriced jobs.

How often should I review my pricing and margins?

We suggest at least once a year, and whenever a significant cost goes up. Labor rates, insurance premiums, material costs, and fuel all shift. Pricing that was profitable last year may be thin today if costs have risen and your rates haven't moved.

The Bottom Line

Margin is the number that actually measures the health of a trade business, not revenue, not how many jobs you ran, not how busy you were. For example, a contractor running 20% net margin on $200,000 of revenue keeps $40,000 in profit. A contractor running 8% on $400,000 keeps $32,000 while doing twice the work.

Know what the published numbers measure. Calculate your own margin the same way. Set a target from your own overhead and goals, and price to defend it. Use the Job Profit Calculator on every job to see the margin before you quote the customer, not after you've committed to a number you can't change.
