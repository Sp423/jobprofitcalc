What Is a Burdened Labor Rate for Contractors?

September 3, 2026 · 6 min read · JobProfitCalc.com

What is a burdened labor rate for contractors? It is the fully loaded cost of one hour of a worker: wage, plus payroll taxes, workers' comp, benefits, and similar payroll burden. It is what that hour actually costs you. It is not what you charge the customer.

Price the job with the wage alone and payroll tax, workers' comp, and benefits come out of profit. This post builds the cost of the hour. It does not set your charge-out hourly rate (that is income-goal math). Overhead (truck, tools, shop) is a third bucket.

Burdened Labor Rate Is Cost, Not What You Charge

Three numbers get mixed on quotes. They are not interchangeable.

Burdened labor rate (this post): the fully loaded cost per hour of you or a crew member on payroll.

Charge-out hourly rate: what you bill. That walkthrough is the post on charge-out hourly rate. This post does not repeat that formula.

Overhead: costs that exist whether a given job is running (truck, tools, shop, general liability, software). See what overhead percentage a contractor should use. Do not dump the truck into labor burden, and do not hide payroll tax inside overhead so it disappears from both.

Formula for a paid hour:

Burdened labor rate = (Wage + Employer payroll taxes + Workers' comp + Benefits + Other payroll burden) ÷ Paid hours

Divide by productive hours (hours actually on the job) and the cost per hour on the tools is higher. Same dollars. Fewer hours in the denominator.

What Goes Into a Fully Loaded Hour

Pull these from payroll and your own policies. Do not copy a "typical burden percent" from a forum. Your class code, state, and benefits are not someone else's.

Wage. Straight-time pay for that person. If they get overtime on this job, use the overtime wage for those hours.

Employer payroll taxes. The employer share of Social Security and Medicare, plus federal and state unemployment. These are not the employee's withholdings. Pull the employer lines from your payroll report. That is not tax advice.

Workers' compensation. Premium tied to payroll for that work. It belongs on the labor hour, not in a pile with the truck.

Benefits. Health insurance you pay, retirement match, and other employer-paid benefits. Convert the monthly or annual cost into an amount per paid hour.

Paid time off. Vacation, holidays, and sick pay still cost you. Add those dollars to the burden, or divide annual labor cost by productive hours. Do not skip both.

Other payroll-tied costs. Uniforms, payroll processing, or mandated paid leave if you treat them as a cost of having that person on payroll.

A Worked Example (Made-Up Numbers)

The figures below are an example only. Round numbers to show the math. Not a claim about pay, workers' comp in your state, or typical benefits.

Example: one crew member at $28 per hour, looking at one paid hour.

Example hour (made-up numbers, not a typical rate)
Hourly wage$28.00
Employer payroll taxes (example)$2.50
Workers' compensation (example)$1.80
Health insurance and other benefits, per hour (example)$4.70
Fully loaded cost per paid hour$37.00
$28.00 + $2.50 + $1.80 + $4.70 = $37.00

The loaded cost is $9 an hour above the wage. Six hours at $28 books $168 of labor. At $37 the labor line is $222. You left $54 out of the job price before overhead, materials, drive, or profit. That $37 is still a cost, not your selling price. It goes into the job as the labor line.

Cost per Productive Hour

You pay for hours that never land on a ticket: shop time, waiting on a delivery, unbilled cleanup, PTO. Recover cost only on hours that hit the job and those paid idle hours have nowhere to go.

Same example person. You pay 8 hours at the $37 loaded rate: $296. They are on the tools for 6.5 hours.

Cost per productive hour = (Loaded cost per paid hour × Paid hours) ÷ Productive hours
$37.00 × 8 ÷ 6.5 ≈ $45.54

The job should see about $46 per hour on site, not $28, and not even the $37 paid-hour rate, if 1.5 paid hours did not hit the ticket. Use your timesheets. If almost every paid hour is on a job, the paid-hour rate is close enough. If a lot of paid time is not on tickets, divide annual fully loaded labor cost by productive hours.

Put the Loaded Rate on the Job

On the estimate, the labor line is hours × workers × burdened rate. Not hours × wage.

Overhead stays its own percentage of direct costs. Walk that rate from your overhead percentage, not from this labor stack. Mixing them into one labor number is how the wage looks covered and the truck still eats the month.

Open the job profit calculator. Enter hours and crew size. Put the fully loaded cost per hour (your $37 or productive-hour figure) in the hourly labor rate field. Then fill materials, overhead, drive, and the margin you want.

The usual slip: you know the wage, you type the wage, and you call the leftover profit. Payroll tax, workers' comp, and benefits were never in the quote. They still leave the account.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a burdened labor rate for contractors?

The fully loaded cost per hour of a worker: wage plus payroll taxes, workers' comp, benefits, and similar payroll burden, optionally divided by productive hours. It is a cost, not the rate you quote the customer.

Is a burdened labor rate the same as my charge-out hourly rate?

No. Burdened labor is what the hour costs. Charge-out is what you bill. Use the charge-out hourly rate post for that number.

Does overhead go into the burdened labor rate?

No. Truck, tools, shop, general liability, and software are overhead. Payroll-tied costs are burden. Keep them separate. Calculate overhead from what overhead percentage a contractor should use.

Should I use wage or loaded cost in the calculator?

Loaded cost. In the job profit calculator, the hourly labor rate field is the cost of that hour on the job. Put wage in and the quote is short by payroll tax, workers' comp, and benefits.

What if I have no employees?

If you have no W-2 payroll, you may not need this stack. You still cannot treat your own time as $0. Price your hour from the charge-out hourly rate method. If you run payroll for yourself, burden those hours the same way you would a crew member.

Are these numbers tax or HR advice?

No. This is an educational estimate so the labor line is not just the wage. Use your payroll reports. Consult a licensed tax professional for your situation.

The Bottom Line

A $28 wage is not a $28 labor cost. Add payroll taxes, workers' comp, and benefits, and the example hour costs $37. If productive hours are less than paid hours, the cost on the job is higher still. Put that loaded cost in the labor line, keep overhead separate, and run the job in the job profit calculator so the price has the real hour in it.
