HVAC Pricing Guide

Service Calls, Installs, and Refrigerant

Price HVAC work from the job in front of you: a diagnostic fee plus flat rate for service, equipment at cost, and your margin on the whole ticket. HVAC has big-ticket work and emergency demand, but the average shop doesn't keep much of it. The ACCA's 2025 study of about 1,000 HVACR contractors found an average net profit of 6%, and the researchers treat 10% as the level that funds growth (ACCA and Farmington Consulting Group, reported by Contracting Business, Dec 5, 2025, US). IRS data put plumbing and HVAC corporations at 4.8% net income on receipts, below electrical at 5.0% (IRS SOI Corporation Complete Report, Table 5.1, tax year 2022, US). Thin margins on big tickets are why pricing from your actual job cost matters more than pricing from habit.

The Three Types of HVAC Work, and Why They Price Differently

Service and repair calls: Diagnostic work, component replacements, minor repairs. Typically short-duration jobs with high labor-to-material ratios. Service calls reward technical expertise and speed. Residential service work can carry 48–58% gross margins before overhead, according to Level, a contractor CFO firm that calls the figure directional (vendor benchmark, updated Sep 3, 2026, US). Net margin is far lower once overhead is paid: the 6% ACCA average above covers the whole shop.

System installations and replacements: New equipment installs, full system replacements, mini-splits. Higher ticket values but more material-intensive and more competitive. Equipment is a large share of the job total, so the margin percentage runs lower even on correctly priced jobs. Level suggests 12–18% net on installs as a healthy target; that is vendor guidance, not a survey of what shops earn (Level, Sep 3, 2026, US).

Maintenance and preventive contracts: Seasonal tune-ups, filter replacements, inspections. Lower per-visit revenue but predictable, recurring income that smooths out the seasonal swings that plague HVAC businesses.

Most HVAC businesses run all three simultaneously. Pricing each correctly requires understanding what it actually costs to deliver each type of work, which is a job cost calculation, not an industry average lookup.

How to Price an HVAC Service Call

A service call has two components that must be priced separately even if they're presented to the customer as a single visit:

The diagnostic/trip charge: The fee for showing up, diagnosing the problem, and providing a repair recommendation. This covers your travel time, fuel, truck cost, and the skilled diagnostic labor before any repair work begins.

Published residential service-call fees we found run from about $50 to $210, and most fall between $85 and $125. That comes from 13 contractor price pages in CA, AZ, NJ, TX, NV, FL, OH, MN, CO, NY, and OK, listed under the flat-rate table below. Most of those pages are undated, so the prices are as posted when we captured them in October 2026. Several waive or credit the fee when the customer goes ahead with the repair. Commercial diagnostics we found ranged from $99 to $389: Comfort Time $99 (Santa Fe Springs, CA), MG HVAC $150 (Manalapan, NJ), Scottish-Tom $275 (Orange County, CA), and Hartzell's $389 for light commercial up to 15 tons (Kingfisher, OK).

After-hours premiums vary widely. We found everything from no upcharge (Smart Home Air & Heat, Orlando, FL) to 1.5 to 2 times standard rates on evenings and 2.5 to 3 times on holidays (A-Class Heating & Air, Sacramento, CA, Sep 4, 2026). Hartzell's posts $309 after hours against $210 standard (OK), and Vinco Mechanical posts a $499 after-hours diagnostic against $199 (Brooklyn, NY). Many shops present it as a flat fee. For example: "After-hours service call fee: $225."

If the customer proceeds with the repair, many HVAC businesses credit some or all of the diagnostic fee toward the repair total. This is a customer-friendly policy that reduces friction at the decision point. If the customer declines the repair, the diagnostic fee stands. You've provided skilled labor and expertise regardless of what they decide to do with the information.

The repair labor charge: Time and expertise applied to actually fixing the problem, beyond the diagnostic. This is billed either at your hourly rate or as a flat rate per repair type. Flat rate pricing is the industry standard for HVAC repair work and is covered in the next section.

What HVAC labor bills at: Consumer guides put HVAC labor at $75 to $150 an hour (HomeGuide, Jul 31, 2025, US) and $100 to $250 an hour (Angi, Aug 3, 2026, US). A-Class Heating & Air quotes $85 to $150 an hour for Sacramento (Sep 4, 2026), and a Brooklyn shop posts $165 an hour (Vinco Mechanical, captured Oct 2026). Those are charge rates. In the calculator, Hourly Labor Rate is your loaded cost per hour, not your charge rate.

If your posted rate sits near the bottom of those ranges, work out what you actually need using our guide on how to calculate your contractor hourly rate before your next service call.

Flat Rate vs. Time and Materials for HVAC Repairs

Time and Materials (T&M)

You charge your hourly labor rate for actual time spent plus materials at cost. In practice, T&M has two significant problems for HVAC service work:

First, experienced technicians are penalized for efficiency. For example, a tech who can replace a capacitor in 20 minutes earns less on T&M than one who takes 45 minutes. Expertise and speed, your most valuable assets, reduce your revenue instead of rewarding it.

Second, customers sometimes resist paying for time they didn't observe. Say a homeowner saw you in the driveway for 90 minutes but watched you work for 40 of them. Some will dispute the labor charge on the difference. Flat rate eliminates this entirely.

Flat Rate Pricing

You charge a predetermined price per repair type regardless of how long it takes. For example: capacitor replacement $285, contactor replacement $320, standard thermostat replacement $295, blower motor replacement $585 to $750 for a PSC motor. ECM blower motors run much higher: one Minnesota shop quotes $900 to $2,500 (Northern One Hour, Aug 4, 2026).

The flat rate includes parts and labor bundled. The customer knows the cost before you begin. You have no incentive to stretch the job: you're paid the same either way. Your efficiency directly improves your effective hourly rate.

Why flat rate wins for HVAC service work:

  • Rewards technician efficiency and expertise
  • Eliminates per-minute labor disputes
  • Gives the customer a clear price before work begins
  • Supports higher effective hourly rates than T&M billing
  • Simplifies invoicing

The ACCA study backs this up: flat-rate shops averaged 7% net against 4% for shops using other pricing methods (Contracting Business, Dec 5, 2025, US).

Refrigerant Pricing in 2026: The R-410A Phaseout Impact

This is the issue every HVAC business needs to price correctly in 2026, because the refrigerant landscape has shifted significantly and many contractors are still pricing refrigerant the way they did three years ago.

What Happened to R-410A

The EPA's AIM Act mandated the phasedown of high-GWP refrigerants including R-410A. From January 1, 2025, new residential and light-commercial air conditioning and heat pump systems can't be installed with a refrigerant of GWP 700 or more, which includes R-410A (88 FR 73098, Oct 24, 2023; 40 CFR 84.54(c)(1)). A rule effective July 27, 2026 lets systems whose major components were all made or imported before 2025 keep being installed (FR 2026-10387, May 26, 2026). New residential systems mostly use lower-GWP alternatives: R-454B (sold under names like Puron Advance) and R-32, with R-466A emerging for specific applications.

R-410A is not banned for service use. Existing systems running R-410A can still be serviced and recharged with R-410A. Supply is not tight right now: in August 2026 ACHR News reported "a significant glut of virgin R-410A" (ACHR News, Aug 12, 2026, US).

How to Price Refrigerant Correctly in 2026

Enter refrigerant at the current invoice cost in Material Cost. It has carrying cost, handling risk, certification requirements, and price volatility.

What R-410A costs right now: A 25-lb cylinder of R-410A sold for about $185 to $225 online on October 3, 2026, which is about $7 to $9 a pound (AAA Refrigerant $184.90, Refrigerants Center $217, SupplyShop $225; US). Your supplier price may differ, so check your last invoice. What contractors charge customers is a different number: Absolute Best Air posts $60 to $115 a pound (GA) and MG HVAC posts $150 a pound (NJ). Put your cost in the calculator, not the customer price.

How to charge for R-410A in 2026:

Structure your refrigerant charge in one of two ways:

Option 1, per-pound pricing: Enter the current wholesale cost per pound in Material Cost. Recovery, handling, and replacement risk belong in the job margin.

Option 2, refrigerant on its own line: List refrigerant on its own quote line, entered at your current invoice cost, and state 'Refrigerant billed at current cost at time of service.' This suits months when supplier cost moves. Put that sentence in your estimate.

For new R-454B and R-32 systems: Enter the current invoice cost, not an old R-410A cost. R-454B costs more per pound: 20-lb cylinders listed at $409 and $449.90 online on October 3, 2026, about $20 to $23 a pound (Refrigerants Center, Twisted Refrigerant; US).

The leak finding rule: Before adding refrigerant to any system, find and repair the leak. This is both an EPA requirement and sound business practice. A recharge without leak repair is a temporary fix that will bring the customer back, but potentially with a dispute about why it needed recharging again so soon. Price the leak search and repair separately from the refrigerant charge.

How to Price an HVAC Installation

Equipment installations (full system replacements, new installs, mini-splits) are higher-ticket jobs with a different cost and pricing structure than service work.

The Components of an HVAC Installation Price

Equipment at cost: The unit itself (air handler, condenser, coil, furnace, heat pump, or mini-split) is your largest cost line. Enter the wholesale cost in Material Cost. Your margin covers profit on that equipment.

Sourcing, storage, delivery, warranty handling, and the risk of a damaged unit are real. They are covered by the job margin.

Installation labor: Priced by system type and complexity. Here is what consumer cost guides tell homeowners to expect for installation labor. These are customer prices, not your cost, and they are national ranges:

Job Type Labor Hours Labor Price to Customer Source
AC condenser replacement 4 to 8 hrs $300 to $1,200 HomeGuide, Aug 18, 2025
Full system change-out No published figure $1,000 to $3,000 HomeGuide, Aug 27, 2025
Gas furnace replacement 4 to 8 hrs $1,500 to $3,500+ HomeGuide, Dec 31, 2025
Mini-split, single zone No published figure $300 to $2,000 Angi, Apr 4, 2026
Mini-split, multi-zone (whole job) No published figure $700 to $3,000 Angi, Apr 4, 2026

We found no published figure for air handler or coil replacement labor on its own, or for each added mini-split zone. Your actual hours depend on site conditions, access, ductwork changes, and crew size. In the calculator, enter your hours and your loaded labor cost, not these customer prices.

Associated materials and supplies: Line sets, disconnect boxes, pad or bracket, drain lines, flue pipe, electrical connections, refrigerant for initial charge, filter, thermostat (if included). Enter each at what you pay.

Permit and inspection fees: Never absorb permit costs. Enter the fee you pay in Material Cost so the price carries it. HomeGuide puts HVAC permits at $25 to $300 depending on the job and the city (condenser, Aug 18, 2025; HVAC replacement, Aug 27, 2025; furnace, Dec 31, 2025; US).

Disposal and haul-off: Old equipment removal and refrigerant recovery take labor, a recovery cylinder, and proper disposal. Put the removal time in your hours and any disposal fee you pay in Material Cost. For scale, consumer guides tell homeowners removal and disposal runs about $50 to $200 (HomeGuide, Aug 27, 2025; Angi, Apr 4, 2026; US).

Before overhead, the same job shows a gross margin of 37.5% ($2,352.20 on $6,279.20, after labor, materials and fuel). That is gross margin, not net. Net is what's left after overhead, and the 25% here holds only if the example's 20% overhead is your real number. If your overhead is higher, enter it and the price goes up.

The equipment, refrigerant, and supplies all go in at cost, with no separate markup. The 25% margin applies to the whole job, materials included.

Warranty Callbacks: What You're Obligated to Do (And What You're Not)

This is a topic HVAC techs and business owners get wrong in both directions: either giving away too much free labor out of misplaced obligation, or damaging customer relationships by refusing to stand behind legitimate work.

Your actual legal obligations are limited. Unless your written contract or written warranty states specific callback terms, you are generally not legally required to return free of charge for any issue that arises after a job is complete. Your professional license requirements may impose some minimum standard of workmanship, but they do not typically mandate free callbacks.

One common approach, the same-issue rule: put a written warranty on the specific work performed. For example: "Labor warranted for 30 days on repairs. If the same component fails within 30 days of repair, we will return and repair at no additional labor charge." Pick the period that fits your work; 30 days is an example.

This policy is professional, customer-friendly, and bounded. It says: we stand behind the specific repair we made. It does not say: we'll return free of charge for any issue at any time.

What is not covered by a reasonable callback policy:

  • A different failure in the same system after repair of an unrelated component
  • A system that has a new problem caused by age, use, or external factors
  • Work performed at the customer's direction against your recommendation
  • Refrigerant loss from a leak that was not repaired (if customer declined leak repair at time of service)

When a manufacturer warranty applies: Carrier, for example, gives 10 years of parts coverage when the equipment is registered within 90 days and 5 years if it isn't. Where registration can't legally be required, the 10-year parts warranty is automatic. Registered owners can instead choose 5 years of parts plus 3 years of labor if the installing dealer participates (not available on new construction) (Carrier warranty, US, captured Oct 3, 2026). Unless the customer has a labor option like that, or you sell an extended labor warranty, labor usually isn't covered. Be clear with customers about the difference between the parts warranty (the manufacturer's) and the labor warranty (yours).

Put your warranty policy in writing on every invoice and estimate. Verbal warranty commitments are unenforceable and unlimited in scope. A written warranty is professional, bounded, and protects both sides.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should an HVAC technician charge per hour?

Consumer guides put HVAC labor at $75 to $150 an hour (HomeGuide, Jul 31, 2025, US) and $100 to $250 an hour (Angi, Aug 3, 2026, US). Set your own rate from your costs and income target, not from those ranges. In the calculator, Hourly Labor Rate is your loaded cost, not your charge rate. Most HVAC service work is better priced flat-rate: in the ACCA's 2025 study, flat-rate shops averaged 7% net against 4% for other methods.

How do I price refrigerant now that R-410A costs have changed?

Enter refrigerant at your current cost per pound times the pounds used. In October 2026, 25-lb R-410A cylinders sold online for about $7 to $9 a pound, and R-454B for about $20 to $23. Price volatility, handling, EPA certification, and replacement risk are reasons to keep the invoice cost current and to hold your margin. Consider listing refrigerant on its own quote line so a cost change is easy to see.

Should I use flat rate or time and materials for HVAC service?

Flat rate is the stronger choice for most HVAC service work. It rewards technician efficiency, eliminates per-minute labor disputes, gives customers a clear price upfront, and typically produces higher effective hourly rates than T&M billing. Time and materials is appropriate for diagnostic work where the scope is unknown before investigation begins. Present it as a diagnostic fee, then transition to flat rate once the repair is identified.

How should I price a warranty callback, and am I obligated to do it free?

Not unless your written warranty says so. Put a written same-issue labor warranty on repairs, for example 30 days, and state it on every invoice. This covers legitimate callbacks on your work without creating an open-ended obligation for unrelated failures. Stand behind what you did, and don't absorb what you didn't.

How does the R-410A phaseout affect pricing on new installations?

New equipment using R-454B, R-32, or other low-GWP refrigerants may carry slightly different equipment costs than equivalent R-410A systems. Some new refrigerant types require updated tooling and handling procedures. Factor both into your installation pricing. Do not price new refrigerant types at old R-410A rates.

The Bottom Line

The average HVAC shop nets about 6% (ACCA, 2025), so there is little room for guesswork. Build the price on actual job costs, not on what the last tech charged or what the customer expects. Service calls should carry a diagnostic fee plus flat-rate repair pricing. Enter equipment, parts, and refrigerant at your current cost and let the margin cover profit on them. Define warranty obligations in writing before the job begins.

Before your next HVAC quote, run the job numbers through the Job Profit Calculator. Enter your labor, parts, equipment, and refrigerant costs, and your target margin. The calculator tells you what to charge the customer to hit that margin, not just to cover your costs.
