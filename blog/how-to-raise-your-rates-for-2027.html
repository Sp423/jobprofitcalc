How to Raise Your Rates for 2027 After Labor and Materials Move

November 17, 2026 · 14 min read · JobProfitCalc.com

To raise your rates for 2027, take one typical job you already priced in 2026, run it again in the job profit calculator with your 2027 labor rate and your 2027 material costs, and keep your overhead percentage and profit margin exactly where they were. The difference between the old price and the new price is your raise. Then tell your customers early, before the new prices show up on a quote they were not expecting.

This guide is written for small home service shops: HVAC, electrical, plumbing, general contractors, landscapers, and excavation outfits. It walks through where each 2027 input comes from, what to do when one is missing, how to handle crews near a state minimum wage, and what to say to customers. Two worked examples show the arithmetic line by line.

Why a round raise is not a plan

Many owners pick a number in December, usually three or five percent, and apply it to the whole rate sheet. The number feels safe because it sounds like what everyone else is doing. The trouble is that it has no connection to your costs. If your labor and materials moved more than the number you picked, you just cut your own margin without deciding to. If they moved less, you raised prices you did not need to raise, and that is the kind of increase customers notice and resent.

The shrinking margin case is the one that costs money, and it happens for understandable reasons. Owners worry about losing bids, they remember a customer who balked last year, and a small percentage feels polite. But a margin you quietly give up in January is gone for every job you run until the next reset. Pricing from a rerun job gives you a raise you can explain, because it is just your new costs carried through the same math you already trust.

The reset, step by step

Block out an afternoon. You need the original quote for one job, the job cost report for that job, your payroll register, your burden worksheet, and whatever supplier increase notices have arrived so far.

  1. Pick one typical 2026 job. Choose the kind of work you do most often, at a normal size, with your usual crew. Skip the oddball job that ran long or the one with an unusual material package.
  2. Pull the 2026 inputs from the original quote and check them against the job cost report. Use the hours, crew size, drive time, and material cost the job actually took if they differ from what you quoted, so the 2026 baseline reflects reality.
  3. Set your 2027 loaded labor rate from your planned 2027 wages and your burden worksheet.
  4. Update Material Cost from the supplier notices you have in hand. Mark any line still waiting on a notice as a placeholder.
  5. Leave Overhead % and Desired Profit Margin exactly where they were for 2026.
  6. Run the job in the calculator with the 2027 inputs.
  7. Compare the 2027 price to the 2026 price. The dollar difference, divided by the old price, is the raise this kind of job needs.
  8. Update your rate sheet and set Quote Valid (days) so December quotes do not run past the January changeover.
  9. Tell customers, in writing, before the new prices start.

If you run several distinct kinds of work, such as service calls and installs, repeat the steps for one typical job of each kind. The raise will often differ between them because the labor and material mix differs.

Start with the labor line, not a percentage

Labor is usually the largest cost on a home service job, so get it right first. Open your payroll register and write down what you plan to pay each person in 2027. That means the raises you have already promised, the raises you expect to give to keep good people, and any wage that a state minimum will force up. Use your own plan, not a national average.

Next, run those planned wages through your burden. Burden is the employer cost that rides on top of every wage dollar, and it differs from shop to shop. The burdened labor rate guide covers how to build that worksheet, so the only job here is to feed it 2027 wages. The output is a loaded cost per hour for each worker. From there, the contractor hourly rate guide shows how to turn loaded cost into the Hourly Labor Rate you enter in the calculator. Use the same method you used for 2026 so the comparison is fair.

If you have not decided on 2027 raises yet, decide before you reset prices. A rate sheet built on last year's wages will be short the moment the first raise hits payroll.

National figures can tell you whether your plan is in a reasonable range, though they are no substitute for your payroll. The BLS Employment Cost Index for September 2026 (Q3), released October 30, 2026, Table 5, shows total compensation for private industry in the Construction industry series up 3.1 percent over the 12 months ended September 2026, not seasonally adjusted. In the same table, the occupational group series "Construction, extraction, farming, fishing, and forestry occupations" rose 3.7 percent over the same period. These are two different series measured two different ways, and both are national. If your planned 2027 raises look far below either one, ask whether you will be able to keep your techs.

Then put the new material costs on the same job

Once labor is set, update Material Cost on the same job. Go through the materials on the original quote and price each line from the supplier increase notices you have actually received. Many distributors send these in the fall with an effective date in January. Where a notice gives a new price list, use it. Where it gives a percentage for a product category, apply it to the lines in that category.

Some notices will not have arrived yet. Do not guess. Keep the 2026 cost for that line, label it in your notes as a placeholder, and plan to run the job again when the notice lands. A guessed number has a way of becoming permanent on a rate sheet, and a placeholder you know about is easier to fix than a guess you forgot you made.

National producer prices are useful context for why notices are arriving. The BLS Producer Price Indexes for October 2026, released November 13, 2026 (USDL 26-1687, news release and Table 1), show final demand construction up 5.5 percent from October 2025 to October 2026, unadjusted, and up 1.8 percent from September to October seasonally adjusted. Materials and components for construction rose 6.2 percent over the same 12 months. All of those October figures are preliminary. They describe the country, not your supplier, so your own notices beat the index every time. If you want a routine for checking open quotes each time PPI comes out, the PPI release day guide lays one out.

Keep the margin instead of shrinking it to stay cheap

With labor and materials updated, leave Overhead % and Desired Profit Margin alone. Your overhead percentage was set from your annual overhead and your annual direct costs; if you have a real reason to revisit it, do that as its own project using the overhead percentage guide, not as a lever to make the 2027 number look smaller. The same goes for margin. The margins by trade guide covers how to choose one. Once chosen, it is the thing the reset protects.

Overhead applies to labor, drive labor, and materials at cost. Fuel is added after overhead. Desired Profit Margin then applies to the whole total cost, so the price comes out at the margin you set. Because overhead rides on labor and materials, a rise in either one raises overhead dollars too, which is exactly what should happen: your office, trucks, and tools cost more to keep running as the work you sell gets more expensive.

Two checks show what the alternatives would have cost. If the shop keeps charging $2,296.00 against the 2027 cost of $1,812.08, profit falls to $483.92 and margin to about 21.1 percent. If it picks a round 3 percent raise instead, the price becomes $2,296.00 x 1.03 = $2,364.88, profit is $552.80, and margin is about 23.4 percent. Both choices leave money behind on every job of this kind for the whole year.

A floor for shops near a state minimum wage

Some shops pay helpers, laborers, or yard staff close to the state minimum. If yours does, the state's 2027 rate sets a floor under your labor line. Three states with posted 2027 rates:

  • Michigan: $13.73 per hour effective January 1, 2026, and $15.00 per hour effective January 1, 2027, according to the Michigan LEO Wage and Hour Minimum Wage & Overtime page (checked November 13, 2026).
  • Colorado: a state rate, where no local minimum applies, of $15.71 for 2027 and $15.16 for 2026, per the Colorado CDLE Division of Labor Standards and Statistics Annual Minimum Wages table (checked November 13, 2026). A CDLE press release dated August 13, 2026 states that "$15.71 per hour is a 3.6% increase from the current minimum wage of $15.16." As of November 13, 2026, the 2027 PAY CALC rules were still proposed, not adopted, with a proposed effective date of January 1, 2027.
  • Washington: $17.73 per hour for most workers for 2027, taking effect Jan. 1, 2027, with a 2026 rate of $17.13, per the Washington L&I Minimum Wage page (checked November 13, 2026). L&I news release 26-17 of September 30, 2026 put it this way: "Washington's minimum wage will rise 3.5 percent next year to $17.73 an hour."

A minimum wage is a floor, not a typical tech wage. Most journeymen and experienced techs are paid well above it, so do not take the state's increase and apply it to your whole price list. What the floor does change is the bottom rung of your pay scale, and that is where wage compression comes in. If your helper gets pushed up and your second year apprentice does not, the gap that rewarded experience shrinks or disappears. A shop that wants to keep its gaps has to move the next rung, and the one after it, which means the floor ends up moving the whole scale. Decide how you will handle those gaps before you set the 2027 loaded rates, because that decision is part of your labor line.

Worked example 2: a Washington shop near the floor, mixed crew as one rate

This is an example, and the shop's inputs are assumed. Say the helper earns the 2026 Washington minimum of $17.13 and moves to $17.73 in 2027, the rates from L&I cited above. Say the shop's burden adds 25 percent to each wage dollar (assumed). That puts the helper at a loaded $17.13 x 1.25 = $21.41 in 2026 and $17.73 x 1.25 = $22.16 in 2027, an increase of $0.75. Say the tech is loaded at $38.00 (assumed), and the shop decides to keep the same dollar gap, so the tech also rises $0.75 to $38.75 (assumed decision). A shop that keeps the same percentage gap instead would move the tech by more.

To enter a mixed crew as one rate, set Number of Workers to 1 and Hourly Labor Rate to the helper's rate plus the tech's rate: $21.41 + $38.00 = $59.41 for 2026 and $22.16 + $38.75 = $60.91 for 2027. One "worker" at the combined rate costs the same as both people for the same hours. Other inputs, all assumed: Estimated Hours 4, Drive Time 0.5, Material Cost $120.00 in both years, Fuel / Travel Cost $15.00 in both years, Overhead % 20, Desired Profit Margin 25%.

What to tell customers, and how long the new quotes stay good

Customers handle a price increase much better when they hear about it before it lands. Send the notice in late November or early December, give a clear effective date, and keep the message short. Your regular customers have been watching prices move in their own lives. The BLS Consumer Price Index for September 2026, released October 14, 2026 (CPI release), showed all items up 3.7 percent over the 12 months ending September 2026, before seasonal adjustment. You do not need to quote that number to them. It simply means your notice will not be the first price change they have heard about this year.

Quotes are the other half of the timing. A quote you write on December 10 with a long validity window can be signed in February at a 2026 price. Before you send anything in December, reset Quote Valid (days) so the quote expires before your new prices take effect, or price December quotes for work scheduled after the changeover at 2027 rates. The quote validity guide explains how to choose the window itself.

Example price increase letter (edit before you send)

Here is a short contractor price increase letter you can adapt. Fill in every bracket and read it aloud before sending.

Dear [Customer name],

Thank you for trusting [Company name] with your [type of work] this year. We are writing early so you have time to plan.

Starting [effective date], our standard rate for [service] will be [new rate]. Our costs for labor and materials have gone up, and we have updated our pricing so we can keep sending experienced people and quality parts to every job.

Any work you schedule and approve before [effective date] will be priced at current rates. If you have a project in mind for next year, we would be glad to talk through timing with you.

If you have questions, call or text [name] at [phone number].

Thank you,
[Your name]
[Company name]

Keep the letter free of percentages and supplier names. A percentage invites a debate about whether it is fair, and naming a supplier puts your relationship with them into a customer conversation that has nothing to do with them.

Situations the single job rerun does not cover on its own

Service agreements and maintenance plans renewing in 2027

Treat a maintenance plan like any other job: pick one typical visit, rerun it with 2027 labor and materials, and price renewals from the result. Read each agreement for its renewal terms and any notice it requires, and send the renewal notice early enough to meet them. Plans that renew in spring or summer still get the 2027 price; the rerun you do now covers the whole year.

Open quotes signed in 2026 for work in 2027

A quote a customer has already signed is a commitment at the price on it. Build the job as agreed. Do record it separately in your job cost report, so you can see what it cost you and avoid confusing that result with your 2027 pricing. For quotes that are still unsigned and will not be built until next year, either let them expire under their validity window and requote at 2027 rates, or rerun them now.

Mixed crew rates

If your typical job runs with a lead and a helper at different wages, enter them as one combined rate with Number of Workers at 1, as in the Washington example. Alternatively, average the two loaded rates and keep Number of Workers at 2. Either works as long as you use the same method for 2026 and 2027.

Overtime

If the typical job you chose regularly runs into overtime, your 2027 loaded rate has to include the overtime premium your payroll actually pays for those hours. The simplest approach is to choose a typical job that does not depend on overtime, then price overtime work as its own line from your payroll records. Michigan's wage and hour page cited above is titled "Minimum Wage & Overtime" for a reason: check your state's rules before assuming how overtime is figured.

Subcontractors whose own rates are moving

If the typical job includes a sub, such as a crane, an insulation crew, or a concrete pump, ask each one for their 2027 number in writing. Put it in the same place you carried their 2026 price on the original quote, so the comparison stays clean. A verbal "should be about the same" is not a number; treat it like a missing supplier notice and keep a labeled placeholder until the written figure arrives.

Owner on payroll

If you work in the field and pay yourself a wage, your 2027 pay belongs in the labor rate the same way your techs' pay does. If your pay sits in overhead because you mostly run the office, leave it there and adjust it when you next review your overhead percentage. Either way, keep it in the same place as last year. Moving it between labor and overhead during the reset makes the old and new prices impossible to compare.

Seasonal shops

Landscapers, excavation outfits, and anyone else with a slow winter have an advantage here: the reset happens while the phone is quiet. Do the rerun in December or January, before the spring rush, and send customer notices before the first spring quotes go out. Supplier notices for seasonal materials may arrive later than others, so plan to rerun the job when the last one lands.

Sanity checks and common mistakes

Before you print the new rate sheet, run these checks.

  • Compare your 2027 loaded labor rate to 2026. If it went down, or stayed flat while you gave raises, a wage or burden input is wrong.
  • Confirm Overhead % and Desired Profit Margin match 2026 exactly. A margin that drifted by a point during data entry hides inside the new price.
  • Check that every material line is either repriced from a notice or marked as a placeholder. Unmarked old prices are the most common gap.
  • Look at the raise in dollars as well as percent. A large percentage on a small ticket and a small percentage on a large ticket can both be right.
  • Rerun one more typical job of a different kind. If the two raises are wildly different, find out why before you publish either.

Several mistakes come up again and again. Applying one round raise to everything is the first; the correction is to rerun a typical job for each kind of work you sell. Lowering margin to keep the price close to last year is the second; the correction is to keep the margin and explain the change to customers instead. Guessing at supplier increases is the third; keep a placeholder and rerun when the notice arrives. Raising every price by the state minimum wage increase is the fourth; that increase changes your bottom rung, not your whole market. Leaving December quotes open into the new year is the fifth; shorten Quote Valid (days) before you send them. Forgetting fuel is the sixth. Pull fuel card statements for the last few months and update Fuel / Travel Cost, remembering that fuel is added after overhead.

For the complete pricing method behind all of this, see how to price a contractor job. Trade pages for HVAC, electrical, and plumbing work show the same calculator set up for those trades. Whatever trade you are in, raising your rates this way means the number you send customers rests on your own costs.
