  <h1>How to Price Sidewalk Snow Removal When It Is All Shovel Work</h1>
  <div class="article-meta">
    <span>November 10, 2026</span>
    <span class="article-meta-sep">·</span>
    <span>14 min read</span>
    <span class="article-meta-sep">·</span>
    <span>JobProfitCalc.com</span>
  </div>
</header>

<section>
  <p>If you are asking how much to charge for sidewalk snow removal, published guides give you a starting range: a Denver contractor's 2026 guide citing Housecall Pro (<a href="https://denversnowremovals.com/sidewalk-snow-removal-cost/">Denver Snow Removals</a>, Denver, CO, published August 20, 2026) lists sidewalk-only clearing at $10 to $30 per visit, and an estimating-tool vendor's U.S. price guide (<a href="https://fastestimatemaker.com/guides/how-much-to-charge-for-snow-removal.html">Fast Estimate Maker</a>, published June 3, 2026, updated September 20, 2026) lists sidewalks, front walks and steps at $15 to $40 per push as their own line on a residential route. For commercial sidewalk snow removal, calculator vendor <a href="https://esticube.com/calculators/snow-removal">Esticube</a> puts the U.S. national average for commercial lots at about $0.55 per linear foot per push (rates updated July 1, 2026). Those ranges are a market check. Your actual price should come from how long your crew takes per 100 feet of walk, a per-visit charge that covers unloading and the drive between stops, ice melt billed on its own line, and a minimum that keeps the smallest stop worth the trip.</p>
  <p>This guide walks a small snow removal, landscaping, or property-maintenance shop through building one per-visit walk ticket from its own time and costs, then shows how to run it through the <a href="/calculator">JobProfitCalc calculator</a>. Every minute count, footage, dollar input, and percent in the worked examples is an assumed example. Replace them with your own timed crew records.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Why walks are a different job from the lot</h2>
  <p>Sidewalk work is priced around hand labor and walk-behind equipment, so the cost that matters is crew minutes rather than truck amortization, which is the core of the <a href="/blog/how-to-price-snow-plowing-per-push.html">per-push plow pricing post</a>. Everything below assumes a crew with shovels, a snowblower, or a small sidewalk machine, and no plow truck on the ticket.</p>
  <p>That difference shapes every number. A walk crew's cost rises almost one for one with the minutes they spend on the concrete, so the job of pricing is mostly a job of timing. Two shops with identical hourly costs can land on very different prices simply because one crew runs a two-stage blower on long straight storefronts and the other is hand shoveling narrow residential walks with lots of steps.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>The pricing process, step by step</h2>
  <p>Here is the full sequence. Each step gets its own section afterward.</p>
  <ol>
    <li>Measure the walks on each property in linear feet and count the steps, ramps, and entries separately.</li>
    <li>Time 100 feet of walk with the tool you actually use on that property, then repeat it over several storms.</li>
    <li>Add the fixed minutes for unloading and loading, plus Drive Time for the share of travel between stops.</li>
    <li>Enter ice melt for the walks at your cost as its own material line.</li>
    <li>Run the numbers in the calculator with your loaded labor cost, overhead, fuel, and margin.</li>
    <li>Set a per-visit minimum so a short walk still pays for the stop.</li>
    <li>Choose a quote shape: a flat price per visit, or a rate per linear foot plus a per-visit charge.</li>
    <li>Recheck your minutes per 100 feet after a deep or icy storm and adjust before the next one.</li>
  </ol>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Time a hundred feet, not the whole route</h2>
  <p>Timing a whole route tells you how long the route took that night, which is useful for payroll and not much else. It mixes long storefronts with short residential walks, blends travel into clearing time, and hides which properties are losing money. Timing a standard 100 feet gives you a unit you can multiply by any property's footage.</p>
  <p>The core formula for each visit is:</p>
  <p>Estimated Hours = fixed minutes per visit + (walk length in feet / 100 x minutes per 100 feet) + minutes for steps, ramps, and entries, all divided by 60.</p>
  <p>Fixed minutes cover the work that happens at every stop no matter how long the walk is: pulling the blower off the trailer, grabbing shovels and the spreader, loading back up, and a quick walk-through to confirm the entry is clear. Walking minutes scale with footage. Steps, ramps, and entries get their own count, because a set of six concrete steps with a railing takes about the same time whether it sits in front of a 40-foot walk or a 400-foot one.</p>
  <p>To get your minutes per 100 feet, mark off a known stretch on a typical property and time the crew clearing it at normal snow depth. Do it with each tool you run, because a shovel crew and a blower crew are two different rates. Then repeat over several storms and keep a simple log: property, footage, tool, snow depth, minutes. After a handful of entries, the average becomes your planning number and the outliers tell you where surcharges belong.</p>
  <p>For crew mixes, such as one person on a blower and one on a shovel handling steps and edges, time the pair together and treat that pair as the unit. Their combined minutes per 100 feet goes into Estimated Hours, and Number of Workers is 2.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>The minutes you spend before the first shovelful</h2>
  <p>A short walk still needs the crew to stop, unload, load, and drive to the next address. If those minutes ride only inside a per-foot rate, a 60-foot walk carries almost none of them and a 600-foot walk carries far too many. Keeping fixed minutes and travel separate is what makes small stops price correctly.</p>
  <p>Put the stop's unload and load time into the fixed minutes inside Estimated Hours. Put the crew's share of drive time between stops into the calculator's Drive Time field, and the vehicle cost of getting there into Fuel / Travel Cost. How to split route drive time across stops is covered in the <a href="/blog/should-contractors-charge-for-drive-time.html">drive time post</a>, so it is not re-derived here.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Steps, ramps and the corners that eat time</h2>
  <p>Linear footage misses most of what slows a walk crew down. Steps have to be shoveled by hand even when the walk is blown. Accessible ramps need careful edges. Entry vestibules, corners around planters, and narrow paths between parked cars all add minutes that a flat per-foot rate will not catch.</p>
  <p>Handle these as discrete minutes per item. Time a set of steps, a ramp, and a typical entry the same way you timed 100 feet, then add those minutes to Estimated Hours for any property that has them. A property with two entries and a ramp simply carries more minutes than a straight walk of the same length.</p>
  <p>Deep snow and ice-bonded walks need the same treatment. When snow is well past your normal depth, or a freeze has bonded a layer to the concrete, your minutes per 100 feet can climb sharply. You have two clean ways to price it. You can use a higher storm-night minutes per 100 feet from your own records, or you can add a depth surcharge, for example an assumed 25 percent on the walk minutes past a stated depth. Either works, as long as the trigger is written into your terms before the season so the customer is not surprised.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Does ice melt go on the same line?</h2>
  <p>Keep ice melt separate. Enter only the ice melt spread on the walks, steps, and entries at your cost in Material Cost, and work out the per-application amount using the <a href="/blog/how-to-price-salt-per-application.html">salt per application post</a>. Keeping it on its own line lets you quote clearing alone on a dry-cold storm and add de-icing when conditions call for it.</p>
  <p>Published guides treat it the same way. The Denver Snow Removals guide (Denver, CO, August 20, 2026) lists a salting or de-icing add-on at $20 to $60 per visit, and Fast Estimate Maker (U.S., June 3, 2026, updated September 20, 2026) lists salt or ice melt after the push at $20 to $50. Treat both as market context for what customers are used to seeing as a separate charge.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Your cost per hour versus what the guides say shops bill</h2>
  <p>This is the most common mistake in snow shoveling prices, so it gets its own section. The calculator's Hourly Labor Rate is your loaded cost for one worker for one hour: wage plus payroll costs and other burden, worked out in the <a href="/blog/burdened-labor-rate-for-contractors.html">burdened labor rate post</a>. The figures in the published guides are what shops bill customers.</p>
  <p>The Denver Snow Removals guide (Denver, CO, August 20, 2026) reports hourly snow removal at $25 to $75, which the page attributes to LawnLove. That is a billed price. Typing a billed rate into Hourly Labor Rate would stack overhead and margin on top of a number that already contains them, and the result would overshoot. Use the published ranges only to sanity check the final price the calculator gives you.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Map the walk ticket to the calculator</h2>
  <p>Open the <a href="/calculator">calculator</a> and fill in the fields this way for one visit.</p>
  <table>

</section>

<section>
  <h2>Worked example 1: one storefront walk visit</h2>
  <p>Every input here is an assumed example. Say a storefront's walks measure 600 linear feet with no extra steps. Say your crew clears at 8 minutes per 100 feet, so the walk takes 6 x 8 = 48 minutes, and the stop carries 12 fixed minutes for unload and load. That is 48 + 12 = 60 minutes, so Estimated Hours is 1.0.</p>
  <p>Assumed inputs: Number of Workers 1. Hourly Labor Rate $34.00, a loaded cost from the <a href="/blog/burdened-labor-rate-for-contractors.html">burdened labor rate</a> method and not a billing rate. Drive Time 0.25 hour. Material Cost $8.00 for ice melt on the walks, sized with the <a href="/blog/how-to-price-salt-per-application.html">salt per application</a> method. Material Markup 0. Overhead % 20. Fuel / Travel Cost $6.00. Desired Profit Margin 25%.</p>
  <ul>
    <li>Labor comes to 1.0 hours x 1 worker x $34.00 = $34.00.</li>
    <li>Drive labor comes to 0.25 hours x 1 worker x $34.00 = $8.50.</li>
    <li>Materials enter at cost at $8.00.</li>
    <li>The overhead base is $34.00 + $8.50 + $8.00 = $50.50.</li>
    <li>Overhead at 20% is $50.50 x 0.20 = $10.10.</li>
    <li>Total cost adds fuel after overhead: $34.00 + $8.50 + $8.00 + $10.10 + $6.00 = $66.60.</li>
    <li>Price at a 25% margin is $66.60 / 0.75 = $88.80.</li>
    <li>Profit is $88.80 minus $66.60, which is $22.20, a margin of $22.20 / $88.80 = 25.00%.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Enter materials at your cost. Your margin already earns profit on materials. If you also want a materials markup, use the optional Material Markup field: it adds profit on top of your margin, and the calculator shows your effective margin. This example leaves Material Markup at 0.</p>

  <h3>Checking the result against the published ranges</h3>
  <p>The $88.80 example price sits well above Denver Snow Removals' $10 to $30 sidewalk-only range (Denver, CO, August 20, 2026) and Fast Estimate Maker's $15 to $40 per push for walks and steps (U.S., updated September 20, 2026). That gap is expected, because both of those figures describe residential walks, and this example is a 600-foot storefront with an hour of clearing. The fair comparison for a storefront is commercial: Esticube's national commercial average of about $0.55 per linear foot per push (rates updated July 1, 2026) works out to 600 x $0.55 = $330.00 for this walk. Use that only as a commercial check, never on a single home walk.</p>
  <p>Read the comparison in both directions. If your calculated price on a typical home walk lands above the residential guides, look first at your fixed minutes and route density, because a scattered residential route is usually where the cost hides; tighten routes or raise your minimum rather than cutting margin. If your price lands far below the guides, check that your Hourly Labor Rate really is loaded and that steps and entries were counted. A price that is low because minutes were missed will lose money all winter.</p>

  <h3>The same visit as a per-foot quote</h3>
  <p>Some commercial customers and property managers want a price per linear foot plus a per-visit charge, which matches the structure Rochester uses when it bills owners (more on that below). You can build it from the same time math by splitting the ticket into three lines. Each line gets the same 20% overhead and 25% margin treatment.</p>
  <ul>
    <li>The per-visit charge carries the 12 fixed minutes and the drive. Labor is 0.2 hours x $34.00 = $6.80, drive labor is $8.50, overhead is ($6.80 + $8.50) x 0.20 = $3.06, fuel is $6.00, for a cost of $24.36 and a price of $24.36 / 0.75 = $32.48.</li>
    <li>Walk clearing carries the 48 minutes. Labor is 0.8 hours x $34.00 = $27.20, overhead is $27.20 x 0.20 = $5.44, for a cost of $32.64 and a price of $32.64 / 0.75 = $43.52, which is $43.52 / 6 = about $7.25 per 100 feet.</li>
    <li>Ice melt stays on its own line. Cost is $8.00, overhead is $1.60, for a cost of $9.60 and a price of $9.60 / 0.75 = $12.80.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>The three lines add back to $32.48 + $43.52 + $12.80 = $88.80, the same as the flat visit. Once you round the walk rate to $7.25 per 100 feet, 6 x $7.25 = $43.50 and the quote comes to $88.78, two cents under the flat price. That rounding is fine, and the advantage is that the same rates now price any walk length: a 300-foot walk is $32.48 + 3 x $7.25 plus ice melt sized for that walk.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>A floor for the short walk</h2>
  <p>A per-foot rate with a visit charge handles most properties, but very short walks still come in thin once you account for how small stops break up a crew's rhythm. A per-visit minimum is the simplest guard. Set it at or above what the calculator says your shortest regular stop costs to serve at your target margin, and write it into every agreement.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Worked example 2: a short walk, then a storm-night revisit</h2>
  <p>Again, every input is an assumed example.</p>

  <h3>Part A: the short walk that hits the floor</h3>
  <p>Say a 100-foot residential walk at the same 8 minutes per 100 feet plus 12 fixed minutes, for 20 minutes, entered as Estimated Hours 0.33. Same $34.00 loaded rate, 1 worker, Drive Time 0.25, Material Cost $3.00 for ice melt, Overhead 20%, Fuel / Travel Cost $6.00, margin 25%.</p>
  <ul>
    <li>Labor comes to 0.33 x 1 x $34.00 = $11.22.</li>
    <li>Drive labor comes to 0.25 x 1 x $34.00 = $8.50.</li>
    <li>Materials enter at cost at $3.00.</li>
    <li>Overhead is ($11.22 + $8.50 + $3.00) x 0.20 = $22.72 x 0.20 = $4.54.</li>
    <li>Total cost is $11.22 + $8.50 + $3.00 + $4.54 + $6.00 = $33.26.</li>
    <li>The calculator price is $33.26 / 0.75 = $44.35.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Say the shop's assumed per-visit minimum is $45. The calculator price falls under it, so the short walk bills $45.00. At that price profit is $45.00 minus $33.26, or $11.74. Notice how much of this ticket is drive labor and fuel: $14.50 of the $33.26 cost happens before anyone touches the snow. That is exactly why the floor exists.</p>

  <h3>Part B: the second pass on the storefront</h3>
  <p>Say the storm keeps going and the 600-foot storefront needs a second clearing the same night. Say snow is deeper, so Estimated Hours is 1.4, and you send 2 workers. Say the storm-night loaded rate is $42.00 to reflect overtime, Drive Time is 0.5 hour, Material Cost is $18.00 for heavier ice melt, Overhead is 25%, Fuel / Travel Cost is $14.00, and margin is 30%.</p>
  <ul>
    <li>Labor comes to 1.4 x 2 x $42.00 = $117.60.</li>
    <li>Drive labor comes to 0.5 x 2 x $42.00 = $42.00.</li>
    <li>Materials enter at cost at $18.00.</li>
    <li>Overhead is ($117.60 + $42.00 + $18.00) x 0.25 = $177.60 x 0.25 = $44.40.</li>
    <li>Total cost is $117.60 + $42.00 + $18.00 + $44.40 + $14.00 = $236.00.</li>
    <li>Price at a 30% margin is $236.00 / 0.70 = $337.14, for a profit of $101.14.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Each clearing in a long storm is its own visit. If the agreement says per visit, the storefront in this example bills the first pass and the second pass separately. Writing that rule down before the season saves the argument after the storm.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Flat per visit or per foot plus a visit charge</h2>
  <p>Both quote shapes come from the same math, so choose by customer. A homeowner or a single small shop usually wants one number per visit, and a flat price with your minimum is easy to read. A property manager with several buildings, or anyone comparing bids across sites with different walk lengths, will often prefer a rate per linear foot plus a per-visit charge, because it scales cleanly and shows the logic.</p>
  <p>Whether that visit price sits inside a seasonal agreement or bills per push is a separate decision covered in the <a href="/blog/snow-removal-contract-vs-per-push.html">contract vs per-push post</a>. For reference only, the Denver Snow Removals guide (Denver, CO, August 20, 2026) lists basic seasonal service at $350 to $600.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>What city walk deadlines mean for your pricing</h2>
  <p>Many cities set deadlines for clearing public sidewalks, and some will send a contractor and bill the owner when the deadline passes. The <a href="https://www.rochestermn.gov/streets-parking-transit/winter-control-snow-removal/sidewalk-snow-removal/">City of Rochester, MN sidewalk snow removal page</a> (no page date shown, checked November 9, 2026) says owners must clear public sidewalks within 24 hours of a snowfall under Section 9-6-2, and when the city sends a contractor the bill is a mobilization charge, which the page lists as $60 for the 2022-2023 season, plus removal based on linear footage and ramps. Treat that as an example of structure rather than a current market rate. It shows a public billing model that matches the per-visit plus per-foot quote shape, with ramps counted on their own.</p>
  <p>For your own pricing, deadlines mainly affect timing. A customer who must be clear within a set window may need a second visit during a long storm, which is exactly the Part B situation above. Check your own city's rules directly rather than assuming they match Rochester's.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Sanity checks before you send the quote</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>Confirm that Hourly Labor Rate holds your loaded cost and not a number copied from a price guide.</li>
    <li>Multiply walk length by your minutes per 100 feet and see if the result matches what the crew actually logs on that property.</li>
    <li>Look for missing steps, ramps, and entries, since those are the minutes most often left out.</li>
    <li>Compare the final price to the published ranges for the right customer type: residential guides for home walks, the commercial per-foot figure only for commercial walks.</li>
    <li>Check that the shortest stop on the route clears your per-visit minimum.</li>
    <li>Make sure ice melt shows up on its own line so it can be dropped on dry storms.</li>
  </ul>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Common mistakes and how to fix them</h2>
  <p>Pricing every walk at one flat number ignores footage. A 60-foot residential walk and a 600-foot storefront are different jobs, so build each price from the walk length and keep the minimum as the only flat element.</p>
  <p>Leaving drive time out makes dense commercial routes look great and scattered residential routes quietly lose money. Put each stop's share of travel in Drive Time and Fuel / Travel Cost on every ticket.</p>
  <p>Using normal-snow minutes on a storm night underprices the work when it is hardest. Keep a separate storm-night minutes per 100 feet, or a written depth surcharge, and use a storm-night loaded rate when overtime applies.</p>
  <p>Folding ice melt into the clearing price makes it hard to explain why the bill changed on an icy week. Bill it on its own line.</p>
  <p>Treating a long storm as one visit gives away every pass after the first. Define a visit in writing and bill each clearing.</p>
  <p>Putting plow truck costs into a walk crew's overhead inflates every sidewalk price. Overhead for walk work should recover the walk crew's own gear: shovels, blowers, spreaders, and the trailer that carries them.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Recheck after the deep storms</h2>
  <p>Your first minutes per 100 feet is a guess dressed up as a number. After every significant storm, compare logged minutes on a few properties against what you priced. If ice-bonded walks or deep wet snow keep running long, raise the storm-night rate or the surcharge before the next event. If a crew gets faster with a better blower, you have room to sharpen bids on long commercial walks. A few minutes of review per storm keeps the whole season's pricing honest. When you are ready, plug your own timed numbers into the <a href="/calculator">calculator</a> and build the next walk ticket from them.</p>
</section>
