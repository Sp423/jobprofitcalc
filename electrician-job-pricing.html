Electrical Pricing Guide

Permits, Panels, and Licensed Rate

Licensed electrical work supports premium pricing because of liability, permitting, and technical complexity — the same reason specialty trades like electrical can command 25–30% margins when the job is priced on real costs. A surprising number of electricians still quote hours times a familiar rate and discover the shortfall after the inspection is scheduled or the panel cover comes off.

This page is electrician job pricing in one place: how to price electrical jobs that include diagnostics, permit time, low-voltage scopes, and panel upgrades, then run the same numbers through the calculator above.

What hourly rate should an electrician charge?

Licensed electricians typically charge $80–$130/hr for direct labor. Specialty work — panel upgrades, commercial service, generator installs — often commands the upper end or above it because of the liability, permitting, and technical complexity involved.

That figure is the labor rate you enter in the calculator, not the whole job price. Overhead, materials, drive time, and taxes still sit on top. If you are building the rate from income and billable hours instead of a market guess, use how to calculate your contractor hourly rate, then come back here and apply it to the job.

Permit pulls and inspection time

Permit pulls and inspection scheduling can add 2–4 billable hours to any panel job. Price this in upfront, not as an afterthought when you're already underway. Code compliance research and inspection coordination are real time — build an allowance into any permitted job before you quote it.

Pass permit fees through (or add a modest handling charge for the admin). Never absorb them. They are a job cost that belongs on the customer's invoice, the same way other trades treat permit and inspection line items.

Keep low-voltage off your licensed rate

Low-voltage and data work typically commands lower rates — quote those scopes separately so they don't drag down your licensed electrical rate. A combined “whole-house wiring” number that blends panel work with doorbell, thermostat, or data cable is how licensed hours get sold at a helper rate.

Diagnostic tools are overhead

Specialty diagnostic tools (thermal cameras, circuit tracers, load analyzers) represent overhead. If you use them on jobs, your overhead percentage should reflect their cost and maintenance. That is the overhead field in the calculator — not a surprise you eat after the invoice is sent.

Panel upgrades and change orders

Panel upgrades almost always uncover additional work once the cover is off. Quote with a clear defined scope and include explicit change order language before signing anything. Price the extra circuits, grounding, or replacement feeders as a change — not as goodwill — using the same calculator: extra hours, extra materials, same margin target. See contractor change orders and scope creep for the paper trail.

If a job requires a return trip for materials, that return is billable time. Include a material-run allowance on any job with custom or specialty parts.

Service calls and trip minimums

Always charge for diagnostic time — your expertise in finding the problem is worth money, not just fixing it. Diagnostic time is often where you earn your real hourly rate. Set and enforce a minimum trip fee for service calls under one hour. Quick calls that eat drive time are where small operators bleed margin. Specialty electrical diagnostics often carry a service-call fee in the same range other licensed trades post before any work begins.

What margin should electrical work hit?

Most experienced contractors target a net profit margin of 15–25% after all costs and taxes. Twenty percent is the industry benchmark. Higher-complexity, licensed trades like HVAC, electrical, and specialty mechanical work often command 25–30% because of licensing requirements, technical expertise, and liability exposure. Below 10% is a danger zone — one unexpected cost can wipe out profit entirely.
