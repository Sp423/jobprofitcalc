Change Order Template for Contractors: Stop Scope Creep in Writing

Updated October 3, 2026 · 13 min read · JobProfitCalc.com

A change order has to say exactly what is being added, removed or swapped, why, what it does to the contract price and the completion date, how the change gets paid, and that work on it doesn't start until both of you sign. The complete template is right below, in a formatted version and a plain-text version you can paste into a text, an email or your invoicing software.

After the template you'll find the on-the-spot test for deciding whether extra work needs a change order, word-for-word scripts for the conversations that come up mid-job, and the paperwork routine that gets change orders signed and paid.

How to Fill In Each Field

Header and numbering

Number change orders per job, starting at 1, and never reuse a number. If a change order gets rejected or replaced, mark it void and keep it in the file instead of deleting it, so the sequence has no gaps. Pull the original contract number and date straight off the signed estimate. That reference is what ties the change order to the agreement the customer already signed, and it's how you'll find it later if anyone asks.

Describing the change

Write the description so a stranger could read it in a year and know exactly what was and wasn't done. "Electrical changes in kitchen" fails that test. As an example, "Add one 20-amp circuit from the main panel to a new GFCI receptacle on the island, run through the basement ceiling" passes it. List quantities, sizes, brand and model numbers, and the room or wall.

Use all three lines. Most changes only add work, but a lot of disputes come from a swap that was never written down, or from a removed item the customer expected to see as a credit. The "Not included" line matters just as much. If you're cutting into a finished wall to run that circuit, say whether patching and painting are part of this change order or not. If they aren't, write them up as their own change order or tell the customer they're doing it.

Reason and who asked

Check one box and fill in the name and date. When the customer asked for the change, write the name of the person who asked, not just "customer." When a hidden condition caused it, take dated photos before you touch anything and note in the field that photos are on file. For an inspector's call, copy the correction notice number or attach a photo of the notice. That one line answers the question you'll otherwise hear at the final walkthrough: "Who said we needed this?"

Price, including when you don't know the price yet

Most change orders are fixed price. You know the work, you price it, you write one number. The table then does the bookkeeping: original contract, plus or minus approved change orders, plus or minus this one, equals the new total. Fill in every line, even on a small change, so the customer always sees the running total and never gets surprised by it at the end.

Sometimes you can't know the price until you're in. The wall is open, the floor joist is soft, and you won't know how far the rot goes until you pull more subfloor. Don't guess a fixed number, and don't write "TBD." Pick one of the other two boxes.

With a not-to-exceed price, you bill what the work actually costs at your normal rates, up to a cap the customer approves now. If the work comes in under the cap, they pay the lower amount. If you hit the cap, you stop and write a new change order before going further. For example, say you write a not-to-exceed of $1,800 for subfloor repair: the customer knows the worst case today, and you aren't stuck eating the overrun.

Time and materials with a cap works the same way, spelled out as an hourly rate plus materials. Use it when the scope itself is uncertain, like tracing a wiring fault through old walls. Keep daily time sheets and receipts, because the customer will ask to see them.

Either way, once the work is done, send a short closing note or a follow-up change order with the actual final amount, so the contract total on file matches what you bill.

Schedule

Fill this in every time, even when the answer is zero. Write "None" in the days field rather than leaving it blank. If the change pushes the finish date, put the new date in writing now. A customer who signed a change order that moved the finish date has a hard time complaining later that you ran late. Count days the work actually adds, including waiting on special-order material, not just the hours on site.

Payment terms

Pick one box. For a small change on a job with progress payments, adding it to the next draw is normal. For special-order material you can't return, collect a deposit on signing so you aren't carrying the cost of a custom vanity the customer later changes their mind about. If the change is big relative to the job, or the customer has been slow to pay draws, ask for payment before the change work starts.

Terms and signatures

Keep the three terms lines word for word. The first keeps your original contract in force, including your warranty, payment and dispute terms. The second is your no-signature-no-work line. The third keeps a customer from sitting on a change order for months and then signing it after material prices have moved. Set a window long enough for them to decide and short enough that your supplier prices still hold.

Get both signatures. Yours shows the price is an offer you stand behind. If two owners signed the original contract, try to get both on any change order that adds real money or time.

When Extra Work Becomes a Change Order

You'll get asked in the driveway, on a ladder, with your hands full. Run these steps in order, and you'll have an answer before you climb down.

  1. Find it in the written scope. Open the signed estimate or contract on your phone. Read the line items, the specs, any drawings, and the exclusions. If the work, or the exact item, is written there, it's part of the job and you do it. If it's listed as an exclusion, it's extra. If it isn't written anywhere, keep going. This is why a tight scope and a clear exclusions list in your estimate make this step quick.
  2. Check what it changes. Does it add or change materials (quantity, grade, brand)? Labor hours? The schedule? Does it need a permit, a permit revision, or an extra inspection? Does it change what your warranty covers? A yes to any of these means it needs a change order.
  3. Figure out who asked. If the customer asked, it's a customer change. If an inspector called for it, get the correction notice. If you missed something you should have priced, that's on you and it doesn't get a change order. Fix it and move on.
  4. Ask whether it was hidden. Could you reasonably have seen this during the walkthrough without opening anything up? If not, and your estimate has language covering concealed conditions, it's a change order. If it was visible and you didn't note it, treat it like step 3.
  5. Make the call out loud and write it down. Tell the customer which it is: part of the job, a paid change order, or a no-charge change order. Then put it on paper the same day, whichever one it is.

Customer add-ons

The extra outlet, the second coat of a different color in the hallway, the towel bar they bought themselves and want you to hang. These are the easy ones. Say yes to the work, tell them it's a change order, and write it up before you start. If they hand you their own materials, add a line to the description saying they supplied it, so your warranty covers your labor and not their part.

Hidden conditions: rot, old wiring, ledge rock

Stop work on the affected area as soon as you find it. Take photos with something in the frame for scale. Show the customer in person if they're home, or send the photos right away if they aren't. Then write the change order with a fixed price if you can see the full extent, or a not-to-exceed if you can't. Rotted sill plate under a door, knob-and-tube wiring behind the plaster you were told was updated, ledge rock where the footing was supposed to go: all of these change labor, materials and usually the schedule.

Don't keep working "just to get it opened up" without telling anyone. The customer should hear about the problem before the cost of fixing it starts running.

Code-required upgrades an inspector calls for

An inspector sometimes calls for work that wasn't in the plan: added smoke alarms, an upgraded bonding jumper, a guardrail where the old deck never had one. Get the correction in writing or take a photo of the notice. Then go back to step 1. If your scope already said the work would meet code and this is something you should have caught when you estimated, it's probably yours to absorb. If it's tied to an existing condition outside your scope, or to a customer change, it's a change order. Write the inspector's name and the notice number in the reason field, and the conversation with the customer gets a lot shorter.

Substitutions when a specified item is unavailable

The tile line is discontinued. The window size is backordered for months. Before you buy a replacement, write a change order that names the original item, the proposed substitute with brand and model, any price difference up or down, and any schedule change. Get it signed before you order. A substitute that's "basically the same" to you might look completely different to the customer standing in a finished bathroom.

Small favors

Sometimes you'll want to just do it: tightening a loose cabinet hinge, moving a box of tile to the garage. That's fine. Decide your limit ahead of time instead of on the spot. Anything that needs material, touches another trade's work, needs a permit, or would be covered by your warranty afterward isn't a small favor anymore, no matter how fast it is.

When you do one, write it up as a no-charge change order, with the price field at zero. It takes almost no time, it shows the customer what you did for free, and it keeps the favor from quietly turning into "you always do that."

Deletes and credits

When the customer takes something out (they decide to keep the old light fixture, or they'll paint the room themselves), write a change order for that too, with the item on the "Removed" line and a deduct in the price section. Don't just skip the work and leave the contract alone. A customer who expected a credit and didn't see one on the final invoice will hold onto that longer than any charge. If you already bought material for the deleted item, note in the description whether it's being returned, kept by the customer, or kept by you.

What to Say When It Comes Up Mid-Job

These are short on purpose. Say them in your own voice, but keep the structure: agree to the work, name it as a change, and say when the paper's coming.

"It's just an hour"

"Happy to do it. It's not in the job we wrote up, so I'll send you a quick change order with the price and what it does to the schedule. Sign it and I'll fit it in [today / tomorrow]."

You didn't argue about how long it takes, because the only question that matters is whether it's in the scope. Treating a small item like any other change keeps the hour from turning into a free day by the end of the job.

"Can you just throw that in?"

"I can do it, but it's extra work and material, so it goes on a change order. I'll have it to you [in the next hour]. If the number doesn't work for you, no hard feelings, we just leave it off."

"No hard feelings" gives the customer an easy way to say no, which makes yes more likely. It also shows you're not trying to squeeze them.

"I thought that was included"

"Let's look at it together." (Open the estimate.) "Here's what we wrote up: [read the line]. This part isn't in there. I can add it with a change order, or we keep going with the job as written. Your call."

Reading the actual line takes it from your word against theirs to a document you both signed. If the estimate really is vague on the point, be fair about it and split the difference or eat it, then tighten that line on your next estimate.

The customer who wants to skip the paperwork

"I do it on every job, even small stuff. It protects you as much as me: you see the price before I start, and nothing shows up on the final bill you didn't sign off on. It's one page and I'll text it to you."

Calling it your standard routine keeps the question of trust out of the conversation. Pointing to their side of it, no surprise charges, is usually enough.

When one spouse asks for something the other didn't

"Sure, I can price that. Since it changes the contract, I'll send the change order to both of you, so you're both seeing the same number before anything starts."

This keeps you out of the middle. Both owners see the change before you do the work, which heads off "I never agreed to that" at the final payment.

A verbal OK on a phone call

"Great, thanks. I'll text you the change order in the next few minutes so it's in writing. Reply 'approved' to the text so I can get started, and sign the form when you get a chance."

Then do it. Send a text or email the same day that repeats the description, the price and the schedule change, and attach the change order. A written reply from the customer is far better than your memory of a phone call, and the signed form closes the loop. Don't let "they said yes on the phone" be your only record.

Logging, Signing and Billing Change Orders

Keep a change order log for every job

One sheet per job, kept with the contract. A spreadsheet or the change order feature in your job software both work. Log every change order, including voids and no-charge ones, with these columns:

  1. Change order number
  2. Date issued
  3. Short description
  4. Reason (customer, hidden condition, inspector, substitution)
  5. Amount, add or deduct
  6. Days added
  7. Status: sent, signed, void
  8. Date signed
  9. Invoice or draw it was billed on

The last column is the one people skip, and it's the one that catches change orders that got signed and never billed.

Getting it signed fast

  1. Keep the template saved on your phone, both as a fillable PDF and as the plain text above.
  2. Fill it in on site while the customer is still there, or right after the call.
  3. Text or email it as a PDF, or send it through an e-signature tool or the e-sign feature in your estimating software.
  4. If you're both on site, have them sign a printed copy or sign on your tablet, and take a photo of the signed page before you leave.
  5. Save the signed copy to the job file and update the log the same day.

When work has to start before it's signed

Some situations can't wait: an active leak, exposed live wiring, a sagging header that needs shoring before anyone stands under it. In those cases, do only what it takes to make it safe and stop the damage. Take photos before and after. Tell the customer right away, by phone if you can, and follow up the same day in writing with what you found, what you did, and why it couldn't wait.

Then write the change order for the emergency work you already did, and a separate change order for the permanent repair. The second one follows the normal rule: no work until it's signed. Splitting them keeps "you had to do it" from stretching to cover a repair the customer never approved.

Bill change orders on the next draw or invoice

Bill signed change orders on the very next draw or invoice, unless the change order says otherwise. Don't save them all for the end. On the invoice, show the original contract amount, each approved change order by number with its add or deduct, the revised contract total, payments received, and the amount due now. Credits go on the same invoice as negative lines. Only bill change orders that are signed. If one is still out, chase the signature first.

Mistakes That Sink Change Orders, and the Fix

Starting the work before it's signed

Once the work is done, you've lost your leverage, and the customer is deciding whether to pay for something they already have. Fix: hold the line on "no signature, no work" for everything except the emergency situations above. If you need to keep the crew busy, move them to work that's already in the scope while the change order goes out.

Descriptions too vague to check

"Extra framing" or "misc. plumbing" invites a fight about what was included. Fix: describe the change as specifically as the best line in your original estimate, with quantities, locations and model numbers, plus a "Not included" line.

Leaving the schedule blank

A change order without a new completion date leaves you exposed when the job runs long. Fix: fill in the days and the new date every time, and write "None" when there's no change.

Lumping several changes together

Several items on one change order means the customer can reject the whole thing because of one item they don't like, and nobody can tell later which piece cost what. Fix: one change, one change order. If several come up at once, send them as separate numbers the same day.

Skipping the credit on deletes

Customers keep track of what they took out. Fix: write a deduct change order for every removed item, the same day it's decided.

Saving it all for the final invoice

A stack of change orders at the end looks like a bill you're springing on them, even if each one was signed. Fix: bill each on the next draw, so the customer sees them land one at a time while the work is fresh in their mind.

Letting the crew say "sure"

A helper agrees to move a light fixture and now the customer believes it's free. Fix: one person on the job authorizes changes. Give the crew one line to use: "Let me grab [Name], they handle anything that's not on the plan."

Not updating the contract total

If the running total on each change order is wrong or missing, your final invoice won't match what the customer expects. Fix: fill in every line of the price table on every change order, and check it against your log before you send it.
