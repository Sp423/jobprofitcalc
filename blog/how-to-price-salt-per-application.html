How Much to Charge Per Salt Application This Winter

October 27, 2026 · 13 min read · JobProfitCalc.com

A national pricing guide from Fast Estimate Maker, "How much to charge for snow removal" (updated Sep 20, 2026), lists salt as its own line item at $50 to $200 per application on a parking lot and $20 to $50 per push on a driveway. That is a published range, not a rate sheet. Where your price belongs inside that range, or above it, comes down to how many pounds one pass uses on a given lot, what you pay per ton this winter, and the labor, drive time, fuel, overhead, and margin wrapped around it.

This guide shows how to price salt per application from your own numbers, then check that price against the published range before you send a quote. It is written for snow removal shops, landscapers moving into winter work, and property maintenance companies that want ice melt per application to cover its cost and still leave profit after the season.

Start with what one pass actually uses

Every salt price starts with one number: pounds put down on that lot in one visit. Multiply that by your cost per pound (or convert to tons and multiply by your cost per ton) and you have the salt dollars that go into the Material Cost field. Everything else in the quote builds on top of that figure, so a guess here throws off the whole price.

The formula is short. Pounds used per visit divided by 2,000 gives tons. Tons times your cost per ton gives the salt cost for that application. If you buy by the bag, the math works the same way once you turn your invoice into a cost per pound, which is covered in the next section.

The hard part is getting the pounds right. There is no single verified application rate to borrow, and product labels vary, so the most reliable source is your own crew. Record how many bags go out on a lot, or note the hopper level before and after, and log it against the lot and the conditions. After a few events you will know what that site really takes.

For the examples in this post, say a shop has measured its own usage at 300 pounds per application on a 20,000 square foot lot. That works out to 15 pounds per 1,000 square feet for this example lot only. It is an assumed example input, not a standard or a recommendation. Replace it with your own measured usage or the rate on your product label.

Measure the area you actually treat, too. A lot listed at 20,000 square feet may have islands, a fenced dumpster pad, or a section the owner never wants salted. Pricing the deeded footprint instead of the treated footprint is one of the fastest ways to overcharge on one site and undercharge on the next.

Where your cost per ton comes from

Your cost per ton should come from your own supplier invoice. Public bids from towns and counties are useful because they show which direction salt is moving, but they are delivered bulk prices for buyers ordering thousands of tons. A shop buying bags or pallets will pay a different number, usually with different delivery terms.

Here is what Illinois buyers reported paying or being offered this season. All of these are Illinois figures. If you work in another region, use them only to understand the trend and price from your own supplier.

Taken together, Illinois municipal bulk prices this season run from $74.33 to $163 per ton, based on the Downers Grove agenda (Oct 13, 2026) and Shaw Local reporting (Sep 15, 2026). That spread is the point. Two buyers in the same state can see prices more than twice apart in the same fall, so a number you used last winter is no longer safe to assume.

The Downers Grove $86.68 figure appears in the worked example below as a stated reference point, because it is a recent, documented delivered bulk price. It is a municipal contract price in DuPage County, Illinois. Your bag or pallet cost will almost certainly differ, and your invoice is the only number that belongs in your Material Cost field.

To turn a bag or pallet invoice into a cost per ton, add up everything you paid for the load, including any delivery or fuel charge on the invoice, and divide by the total pounds received. That gives cost per pound. Multiply by 2,000 for cost per ton. Doing it this way keeps delivery charges in the salt cost instead of losing them.

Put salt on its own line, separate from the push

Salt belongs on the estimate as its own line. When it is buried inside a per-push price, two problems follow. The customer cannot see what they are paying for, so every price change looks like a jump in your plowing rate. And you cannot change the salt price alone when your supplier raises the cost per ton halfway through winter.

A separate line also matches how the published range is framed. Fast Estimate Maker's guide (updated Sep 20, 2026) lists salt as a line item per application, apart from the plowing itself. Keeping the same structure makes it easier for a property manager to compare your quote with others.

If you are weighing a seasonal contract against per-push billing, or need wording for a salt pass-through clause, that is covered in seasonal snow contracts versus per-push pricing.

Set a per-application minimum so small lots still pay

On a small lot, the salt itself is a minor part of the cost. The truck still has to leave the yard, the crew still drives there and back, and the visit still takes a slot on the route during the busiest hours of a storm. If you price a small lot strictly by pounds used, the number can come out low enough that the stop barely covers its own drive.

A per-application minimum fixes that. It sets the lowest price you will charge for any salt visit, no matter how few pounds go down. Run the calculator on your smallest typical lot to find its floor, then set a minimum at or above that floor so every stop on the route carries its share. Worked Example 2 shows how this changes the price on a very small lot.

Why salt prices moved this year, and why a short quote window helps

Shaw Local reported on Sep 15, 2026 that Illinois towns had to scramble for road salt after the state bid went unfilled. In the same report, St. Charles, Illinois secured salt at a 53% cost increase from last year, and one Illinois official received bids of $120 and $163 per ton against roughly $80 per ton in previous years. Shaw Local had reported a month earlier, on Aug 13, 2026, that Yorkville, Illinois paid $111.41 per ton versus $72 per ton the year before.

When the supply side is that unsettled, a quote that stays valid for months is a risk. If your supplier raises the price in January and your quote from October is still open, you absorb the difference on every application. A shorter validity window on salt pricing protects you. See how long a contractor quote should stay good for how to set it.

How to price one salt application, step by step

  1. Measure the area you will actually treat, leaving out islands, landscaped beds, and any section the owner excludes.
  2. Record the pounds your crew uses on that lot per application, from bag counts or hopper readings across several events.
  3. Get your cost per ton from your latest supplier invoice, including any delivery charge you paid.
  4. Convert pounds to tons by dividing by 2,000, multiply by your cost per ton, and enter that figure as Material Cost.
  5. Add on-site hours, crew size, your loaded labor rate, drive hours, and fuel for the visit.
  6. Enter your overhead percentage and desired profit margin, then run the calculator to get your floor price.
  7. Compare the floor price with the published per-application range and decide where your quote should land.
  8. Check the result against your per-application minimum and charge whichever is higher.

Worked Example 1: one application on a small commercial lot

This is an example. Every input below is assumed, and you should replace each one with your own numbers.

Say the lot is 20,000 square feet of treated surface. Say the shop's own measured usage on this lot is 300 pounds per application; this is an assumed example input, not a standard rate. For cost per ton, this example uses $86.68 per ton as a stated reference point, which is the Morton Salt price on the State of Illinois contract reported in the Village of Downers Grove, Illinois Council agenda dated Oct 13, 2026. Your own invoice will differ.

The remaining assumed inputs: one worker, 0.5 hours on site, a loaded labor rate of $40.00 per hour, 0.25 hours of round trip drive time, $6.00 in fuel, 20% overhead, and a 30% desired profit margin.

First, the salt cost. 300 pounds divided by 2,000 is 0.15 tons. 0.15 tons times $86.68 comes to $13.00, rounded to the cent.

LineArithmetic and result
Labor0.5 hours x 1 worker x $40.00 = $20.00
Drive labor0.25 hours x 1 worker x $40.00 = $10.00
Materials at cost0.15 tons x $86.68 = $13.00
Overhead base$20.00 + $10.00 + $13.00 = $43.00
Overhead20% x $43.00 = $8.60
Fuel$6.00
Total cost$20.00 + $10.00 + $13.00 + $8.60 + $6.00 = $57.60
Price$57.60 / (1 minus 0.30) = $57.60 / 0.70 = $82.29
Profit$82.29 minus $57.60 = $24.69
Margin earned$24.69 / $82.29 = 30.0%

Enter materials at your cost. Your margin already earns profit on materials. If you also want a materials markup, use the optional Material Markup field: it adds profit on top of your margin, and the calculator shows your effective margin. This example leaves Material Markup at 0.

If you want to read more about when a materials markup makes sense, see how to mark up materials as a contractor.

Now compare. The calculator floor for this example is $82.29. Fast Estimate Maker's national guide (updated Sep 20, 2026) lists $50 to $200 per application on a lot. The example price sits inside that published range, toward the lower half. That tells the shop its price is not out of line with what others publish, and that it has room to quote higher if the lot is hard to reach, has steep entrances, or needs service during peak storm hours.

Notice how small the salt line is relative to the whole price. At this example cost per ton, salt is $13.00 of a $57.60 total cost. Labor, drive time, and overhead make up most of the cost of the visit, which is why a lot that uses very little salt can still carry a meaningful price.

Worked Example 2: when the shop's salt cost goes up

Same lot, same assumed usage, same crew, hours, loaded rate, drive time, fuel, overhead, and margin. The only change is the cost per ton. Say the shop's supplier now charges $111.41 per ton, which matches the price Yorkville, Illinois paid Midwest Salt as reported by Shaw Local on Aug 13, 2026. It is used here as a labeled reference; your own supplier price will differ.

Salt cost: 0.15 tons times $111.41 comes to $16.71, rounded to the cent.

The price goes from $82.29 to $88.64, an increase of $6.35 per application. That is the whole effect of the higher salt price on this example lot once overhead and margin are carried through.

Now look at what happens if the shop keeps charging the old price. At $82.29 with the new total cost of $62.05, profit falls to $82.29 minus $62.05, which is $20.24. Margin earned drops to $20.24 divided by $82.29, or 24.6%. Spread across every application on every lot for the rest of the season, that gap adds up. This is why salt sits on its own line and why the quote window matters.

The minimum does what it is meant to do: the small stop now pays for its place on the route. The $75.00 figure is an example; set your own minimum from your own smallest typical lot and your market.

When your floor price lands above the published range

Sometimes the calculator gives a floor above $200 per application on a lot, the top of Fast Estimate Maker's published national range (updated Sep 20, 2026). The price may still be right, so check a few things before you either send it or cut it.

Recheck the pounds first. If usage came from one bad storm or a crew that overapplies, the salt line may be inflated. Then recheck the cost per ton against your latest invoice. Look at drive time next: a lot far off your route can carry a large drive labor and fuel cost, and pairing it with nearby work may be a better fix than lowering the price. Finally, confirm your overhead percentage is current and not padded.

If every input holds up, the price is your real cost plus your margin. Quoting below it means doing the work at a lower margin than you chose, or at a loss. In that case you can quote the floor and explain the salt line, offer a seasonal arrangement, or pass on the lot. Large lots in particular can legitimately run above a published range built for typical sites.

Edge cases that change the price

A storm that needs a second application

Long or heavy events can need salt more than once. Price each application as its own visit with its own pounds, time, drive, and fuel. If the crew stays on site between passes, the second pass may carry no added drive time, so adjust the drive hours instead of copying the first visit.

A refreeze call with no plowing

Melt and refreeze cycles often bring salt-only calls the next morning. With no push to share the trip, the full drive time and fuel sit on the salt visit alone. This is exactly where the per-application minimum earns its keep.

A lot that only needs the entrances

Some owners want only the entrances, drive lanes, or a loading dock treated. Measure just that area and record usage for that scope. The salt line will drop, while drive time and setup stay the same, so the minimum will often set the price.

A supplier price change mid-season

When a new invoice comes in at a different cost per ton, rerun the calculator for your typical lots that day. Because salt is its own line, you can update it on your next quote without reworking the plowing price. If your existing agreements include a pass-through clause, the wording for that lives in the contract post linked earlier.

Bag salt versus bulk

Bag salt usually costs more per pound than bulk, and it is easy to underestimate when you think in bags instead of pounds. Convert every invoice to cost per ton using total paid divided by total pounds, times 2,000. If you mix bag and bulk on the same route, price each lot with the product it actually gets.

Sanity checks and common mistakes

  • Using a town's delivered bulk contract price as your own cost per ton understates your salt line; use your own supplier invoice instead.
  • Guessing pounds from lot size alone gives a shaky Material Cost; record actual usage per lot over several events and update it.
  • Entering your billing rate in the Hourly Labor Rate field double counts margin; enter your loaded cost per hour.
  • Leaving drive time out of a salt-only visit makes refreeze calls look profitable when they are not; enter the round trip hours every time.
  • Putting spreader and truck costs into Material Cost mixes ownership costs with salt; keep them in overhead.
  • Keeping last winter's salt price on this winter's quotes hides a cost increase that, in Illinois, ran as high as 53% for St. Charles according to Shaw Local on Sep 15, 2026; rerun the numbers with your current invoice.
  • Quoting salt inside a single per-push number makes mid-season changes hard to explain; list it separately.
  • Pricing small lots purely by pounds leaves them underpaid; compare against your per-application minimum before sending the quote.

One quick check before any quote goes out: look at the salt line as a share of total cost. If it looks far out of proportion compared with your other lots, the pounds or the cost per ton probably need a second look.

Run your own numbers

Salt application pricing gets easier once you have three things on hand for each lot: measured pounds per visit, a current cost per ton from your own invoice, and your loaded labor and overhead figures. Put those into the calculator, compare the result with the published per-application range, apply your minimum, and you will know what to charge to salt a parking lot before the first storm instead of after it.
