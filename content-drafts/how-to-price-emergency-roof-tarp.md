<section>
  <p>Published 2026 guides put the emergency roof tarp cost for a small job at $350 to $600, plus an after-hours add of $150 to $300, according to <a href="https://squaresforsales.com/blog/emergency-roof-repair-pricing">Squares for Sales</a>, a contractor-written price guide from a roof-measurement software vendor (U.S., Nashville, TN author, updated August 15, 2026). A <a href="https://roofershotline.com/emergency-roof-repair/">Roofers Hotline</a> summary of published 2026 guides (U.S., updated October 17, 2026) puts emergency roof tarping cost at $200 to $1,000 per job. The way to land inside those ranges and still make money is to price the tarp call as one ticket built from your after-hours labor cost, tarp materials and drive time, then quote the daylight leak fix as a second ticket with its own inspection line.</p>
  <p>This guide walks a roofing or small exterior shop through both tickets: what goes into the night visit, why the published 2-hour labor minimum matters, how to bill for finding the leak, where the roof scope stops, and how to run each visit through the <a href="/calculator">JobProfitCalc calculator</a> with worked numbers you can check line by line.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Why the tarp and the fix are two different jobs</h2>
  <p>An emergency roof call usually involves two trips with two different goals. Visit 1 happens at night or on a weekend, and its only job is to stop water from coming in. The crew climbs, covers the damaged area, battens the tarp down, and leaves. Nobody is tearing off shingles in the dark to hunt for a cracked pipe boot.</p>
  <p>Visit 2 happens in daylight. The crew pulls back the tarp, traces where the water actually entered, and repairs it. That visit has a different labor cost, a different set of materials, and a different kind of uncertainty, because the cause of the leak is unknown until someone looks.</p>
  <p>Quoting both as a single number forces you to guess the repair before you have seen it, at night, on the phone. If the guess is low, you eat the difference. If it is high, the customer hears a scary number at the worst possible moment. Two tickets fix both problems. The tarp quote is firm and fast. The repair quote comes after the inspection, when you know what you are fixing.</p>
  <p>Plumbers face the same shape with burst pipes: shut off and stabilize first, repair second. The <a href="/blog/how-to-price-burst-pipe-repair.html">burst pipe pricing guide</a> shows the sister version of this two-ticket approach for that trade.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>What goes into a tarp visit at night</h2>
  <p>A tarp ticket has fewer moving parts than most roofing quotes, which is exactly why each part needs to be counted. Here is what the night visit carries.</p>
  <h3>The after-hours labor cost</h3>
  <p>Your crew costs more at 10 p.m. than at 10 a.m., whether through overtime, on-call pay, or simply the price of getting someone to answer the phone. That higher cost belongs in the Hourly Labor Rate field for this ticket. The method for setting an after-hours rate is covered in the <a href="/blog/after-hours-rate-for-contractors.html">after-hours rate guide</a>, so this post takes that rate as given and does not rebuild it.</p>
  <p>One distinction trips up many shops. The published figures in this article are what roofers bill customers, or ranges collected by guides. The calculator's Hourly Labor Rate is your shop's loaded cost per hour: wages plus payroll costs and the other per-hour costs of employing someone. Use the published billed ranges only as a market check on the final price, never as the labor input. If you type a billing rate into a cost field, you will stack margin on top of margin and price yourself out of the call.</p>
  <h3>Hours, crew, and the minimum</h3>
  <p>Estimated Hours should reflect a labor minimum, covered in the next section. Number of Workers matters more on a tarp call than on many daytime jobs, because tarping a steep or wet roof at night is commonly a two-person task: one on the roof, one at the ladder handing up battens and fasteners. Each added worker multiplies both the on-site hours and the drive time.</p>
  <h3>Tarp, battens, and fasteners</h3>
  <p>Squares for Sales (U.S., Nashville, TN author, updated August 15, 2026) lists materials on a tarp quote as the tarp, fasteners, and batten lumber. Enter what those actually cost your shop in Material Cost. Pull the number from your last restock rather than guessing, and include the batten lumber even if it came off a scrap pile, because that pile was paid for once already.</p>
  <h3>Getting there and back</h3>
  <p>A night call is a dedicated trip with no other stops to share the drive. Put the round-trip hours in Drive Time and the vehicle cost in Fuel / Travel Cost. Whether and how to charge for drive time is argued out in the <a href="/blog/should-contractors-charge-for-drive-time.html">drive time guide</a>; this post assumes you count it. Squares for Sales also lists a trip charge outside the service area as part of a tarp quote, which is the same idea applied to longer runs.</p>
  <h3>Overhead and margin</h3>
  <p>Overhead % covers the costs that keep the shop running, such as the truck note, the phone line that rang at night, and office time spent on dispatch. Desired Profit Margin is your profit as a share of the final price. On an emergency ticket, the premium shows up in two visible places: the higher after-hours labor cost and the margin you choose. There is no hidden multiplier anywhere in the math, which makes the quote easy to defend line by line.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>The minimum that covers a short climb</h2>
  <p>Some tarp jobs take forty minutes on the roof. If the quote covers only forty minutes, the shop loses money on every short call, because the phone call, the loading, the setup, and the teardown all happen regardless of how small the hole is.</p>
  <p>Squares for Sales (U.S., Nashville, TN author, updated August 15, 2026) states that its tarp quote includes a 2-hour labor minimum, along with materials, an emergency surcharge, and a trip charge outside the service area. That 2-hour figure is the published minimum used in this article. Treat it as one guide's practice, not a rule, and adjust if your own job history says otherwise.</p>
  <p>In the calculator, the minimum simply means Estimated Hours never goes below 2.0 on a tarp ticket, even when the climb is short. If a job runs longer, enter the real hours. Anything above 2.0 in the examples below is an assumed input, labeled as such.</p>
  <p>Squares for Sales also sorts tarp jobs by size: small at $350 to $600, medium (6 to 20 sq ft) at $600 to $1,000, and large (20+ sq ft) at $1,000 to $2,500+. Those are billed prices, so they tell you where the market sits for each size, not how many hours to enter.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Finding the leak is billable time</h2>
  <p>The second visit opens with work the customer often does not expect to pay for: finding the leak. Water can travel along decking or rafters before it shows up on a ceiling, so the hole in the drywall and the hole in the roof may be several feet apart. Tracing that path takes skill and time.</p>
  <p>Quote that inspection as its own line, separate from the calculator ticket for the repair. Whether to waive it, credit it, or hold it firm is a policy question answered in the <a href="/blog/should-contractors-waive-diagnostic-fee.html">diagnostic fee guide</a>. For reference, <a href="https://mmroofrepair.com/blog/what-does-emergency-roof-repair-really-cost-in-2026/">McGilvary</a>, one Florida roofer's published ranges (updated July 14, 2026), lists an emergency service call fee of $150 to $350 covering dispatch, travel, and assessment.</p>
  <p>Once the cause is known, the repair itself goes through the calculator: hours to make the fix, crew size, repair parts at cost, a second round of drive time and fuel, overhead, and margin. Because this visit happens in daylight, the Hourly Labor Rate drops back to your normal loaded daytime cost.</p>
  <p><a href="https://weathershieldroofers.com/blog/roof-leak-repair-cost-guide-2026/">WeatherShield Roofing</a>, a roofing contractor page for Myrtle Beach, SC (updated June 22, 2026), reports that most leak repairs run $150 to $1,500 and that a pipe boot repair runs $150 to $400. That spread is wide because leak causes vary so much, which is one more reason the repair quote should wait for the inspection. Good roof leak repair pricing starts with knowing what failed.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Where the roof ends and the drywall starts</h2>
  <p>Water damage rarely stops at the roof deck. Wet insulation, stained or sagging drywall, interior cleanup, and soft decking all show up on emergency calls, and none of them belong inside a tarp or leak repair ticket. Quote each as its own line, or refer it out to a shop that does that work, and handle anything discovered mid-job the way the <a href="/blog/contractor-change-order-scope-creep.html">change-order scope creep guide</a> describes.</p>
  <p>Ice dams are a separate pricing problem with their own causes and fixes, and they fall outside this post.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Pricing both visits step by step</h2>
  <ol>
    <li>Set the after-hours loaded labor cost for Visit 1 using the method in the after-hours rate guide, and keep your daytime loaded cost ready for Visit 2.</li>
    <li>Apply the 2-hour labor minimum, so Estimated Hours on the tarp ticket is never lower than 2.0.</li>
    <li>Enter the tarp, battens, and fasteners in Material Cost at what they cost your shop.</li>
    <li>Add round-trip Drive Time in hours and the vehicle cost in Fuel / Travel Cost.</li>
    <li>Run Visit 1 in the calculator with your overhead and margin, then compare the price with the published tarp ranges.</li>
    <li>Quote the diagnostic or inspection fee on Visit 2 as a separate line the customer sees before work starts.</li>
    <li>After the inspection, estimate the hours to make the repair with the crew you will actually send.</li>
    <li>Enter repair parts at cost in Material Cost for the Visit 2 ticket.</li>
    <li>Run Visit 2 as its own calculator ticket with daytime labor cost, its own drive time and fuel, overhead, and margin.</li>
    <li>Put drywall, interior cleanup, and any decking or structural work on their own lines, or refer them out.</li>
  </ol>
</section>

  <p>Here is how the calculator builds the number. Labor is hours times workers times your loaded rate, and drive labor is drive hours times workers times that same rate. Overhead applies to labor, drive labor, and materials at cost, and fuel is added after overhead. Margin then applies to the whole total cost, so the price is total cost divided by one minus your margin.</p>

  <h2>Worked example 1: a small tarp after hours</h2>
  <p>This is an example. Every input below is assumed except the 2-hour labor minimum, which comes from Squares for Sales (U.S., Nashville, TN author, updated August 15, 2026).</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th>Calculator field</th><th>What to enter</th></tr>

      <tr><td>Hourly Labor Rate</td><td>$55.00 (assumed loaded after-hours cost)</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Drive Time</td><td>0.5 hour (assumed round trip)</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Material Cost</td><td>$85.00 (assumed cost of tarp, battens, and fasteners)</td></tr>

  <p>Enter materials at your cost. Your margin already earns profit on materials. If you also want a materials markup, use the optional Material Markup field: it adds profit on top of your margin, and the calculator shows your effective margin. This example leaves Material Markup at 0.</p>

      <tr><td>Labor</td><td>2.0 hours x 2 workers x $55.00 = $220.00</td></tr>

  <p>The calculated price of $687.69 can now be checked against published billed ranges:</p>
  <ul>
    <li>Squares for Sales (U.S., Nashville, TN author, updated August 15, 2026) lists a small tarp job at $350 to $600 plus an after-hours add of $150 to $300, which combines to $500 to $900, and the example sits inside that band.</li>
    <li>McGilvary (Florida, updated July 14, 2026) lists temporary emergency intervention including tarping at $300 to $1,500, so the example sits comfortably inside.</li>
    <li>WeatherShield Roofing (Myrtle Beach, SC, updated June 22, 2026) lists an emergency tarp or temporary patch at $300 to $600, and the example runs above that range on its own.</li>
    <li>The Roofers Hotline summary of published 2026 guides (U.S., updated October 17, 2026) puts emergency tarping at $200 to $1,000 per job, and the example falls inside.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>If your calculated price lands below these ranges, look for missing cost before celebrating a competitive quote: a daytime rate typed in for a night job, the 2-hour minimum skipped, a one-person crew on paper when two climbed, or Drive Time left blank. If your price lands well above the ranges, check whether crew size, hours, or Material Cost are inflated before cutting margin, because margin is the last place to find the gap. Landing above one guide and inside three others, as this example does, is normal; the WeatherShield range reads as a daytime base, and the same page lists a separate emergency after-hours add of $200 to $500.</p>

<section>
  <h2>Edge cases that change the quote</h2>
  <h3>A short climb that still hits the minimum</h3>
  <p>A single missing shingle near the eave might take half an hour to cover. Keep Estimated Hours at 2.0 anyway. The minimum pays for the call, the load-out, the setup, and the cleanup, which all happen no matter how small the patch is. If a customer questions it, the honest answer is that every night call carries that fixed time.</p>
  <h3>A medium or large tarp area</h3>
  <p>When the damaged area grows into the medium or large bands that Squares for Sales describes (6 to 20 sq ft, and 20+ sq ft), raise Estimated Hours above the minimum and raise Material Cost for the larger tarp and extra battens. You may also add a third worker. Each change flows through overhead and margin automatically, so the price climbs in proportion to the work instead of by a guessed lump sum.</p>
  <h3>A second leak found on Visit 2</h3>
  <p>Pulling the tarp sometimes reveals a second failure, such as flashing at a wall or a nail pop several feet away. Stop and quote it before fixing it. Run it as an added line or a second calculator ticket with its own hours and parts, and get approval in writing. Folding it silently into the pipe boot price wipes out the margin on both repairs.</p>
  <h3>Soft decking discovered under the tarp</h3>
  <p>Soft or rotted decking changes the scope from a repair to a section replacement. Document it with photos, quote it as a change order, and do not start until the customer agrees. Leaving the tarp in place while the decking quote is approved keeps the house dry and keeps the original tarp ticket intact.</p>
  <h3>A call that only needs a temporary seal</h3>
  <p>Some night calls turn out to need sealant or a small patch instead of a full tarp. The labor minimum still applies, and Material Cost simply drops to what the patch materials cost. WeatherShield Roofing groups emergency tarps and temporary patches together at $300 to $600 (Myrtle Beach, SC, updated June 22, 2026), which suggests customers see both as the same kind of stopgap. Price it the same way and quote the permanent fix as Visit 2.</p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>Sanity checks and common mistakes</h2>
  <h3>Before you send either quote</h3>
  <ul>
    <li>Confirm the Hourly Labor Rate is a loaded cost and that the night ticket uses the after-hours cost while the day ticket uses the daytime cost.</li>
    <li>Make sure Visit 1 shows at least 2.0 Estimated Hours.</li>
    <li>Check that each visit carries its own Drive Time and Fuel / Travel Cost, since they are two separate trips.</li>
    <li>Verify the inspection fee appears as its own line and does not also include travel already counted in the calculator.</li>
    <li>Compare the final price with the published ranges and be able to explain any gap.</li>
  </ul>
  <h3>Mistakes and their corrections</h3>
  <p>The most common mistake is typing a published billed rate into the labor field. Those numbers already include someone else's overhead and profit. Correct it by entering your own loaded cost and letting the calculator build the price.</p>
  <p>The second is quoting the tarp and the repair as one number over the phone. That forces a guess about damage nobody has seen. Correct it by committing only to the tarp price at night and quoting the repair after the daylight inspection.</p>
  <p>A third is adding a hidden emergency multiplier on top of an after-hours rate that already carries the premium. That double-charges the customer and makes the quote impossible to explain. Correct it by keeping the premium in two visible places, the labor cost and the margin.</p>
  <p>A fourth is giving away the inspection because the customer already paid for the tarp. Finding the leak is skilled work on its own visit. Correct it by quoting the inspection as a separate line and deciding in advance whether you credit it toward the repair.</p>
  <p>The last is letting drywall, cleanup, or decking creep into the roof ticket. Correct it by naming those items as separate lines or referrals the moment they show up, before any work begins.</p>
